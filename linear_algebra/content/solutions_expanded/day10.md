### Exercise 7 — $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ iff $A$ is singular

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **7.** ($\Rightarrow$) If $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, then by Theorem 10.1, $\det(A - 0\cdot I) = \det(A) = 0$, so $A$ is singular (by Day 8's theorem). ($\Leftarrow$) If $A$ is singular, $\det(A) = 0$, i.e. $\det(A - 0\cdot I) = 0$; by Theorem 10.1 (applied with $\lambda = 0$), $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$. Both directions hold, so $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if and only if $A$ is singular. | **The idea in one line.** The eigenvalue $0$ is not a special case needing new tools — it is just Theorem 10.1 read at $\lambda = 0$, where the determinant test $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ collapses into $\det(A)=0$. <br> **Why $\lambda=0$ is so clean.** Setting $\lambda = 0$ makes $A - \lambda I = A - 0\cdot I = A$, so the whole "eigenvalue" test degenerates into "is $\det A = 0$?" — which is exactly Day 8's definition of singular. Both directions of the "iff" are therefore the *same* equivalence chain, just walked forwards and backwards. <br> **What it means geometrically.** $0$ being an eigenvalue means $Av = 0v = 0$ for some nonzero $v$ (Definition 10.1): $A$ crushes a whole direction down to the origin. A map that flattens a nonzero direction to zero cannot be undone, so it has no inverse — that *is* singularity. Eigenvalue $0 \Leftrightarrow$ nontrivial kernel $\Leftrightarrow$ singular. <br> **What just happened:** eigenvalue $0$ is the statement "$A$ kills a direction," and killing a direction is precisely what it means for $A$ to be singular. |

### Exercise 8 — eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal entries

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** Say $A$ is upper triangular (lower triangular is identical by symmetry) with diagonal entries $a_{11}, \dots, a_{nn}$. Then $A - \lambda I$ is also upper triangular, with diagonal entries $a_{11}-\lambda, \dots, a_{nn}-\lambda$ (subtracting $\lambda$ only changes the diagonal). The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries (Day 8), so $p_A(\lambda) = \det(A-\lambda I) = (a_{11}-\lambda)(a_{22}-\lambda)\cdots(a_{nn}-\lambda).$ By Theorem 10.1, the eigenvalues of $A$ are exactly the roots of $p_A$, and the roots of this product are precisely $\lambda = a_{11}, \dots, a_{nn}$ — the diagonal entries of $A$. | **Why the shape survives.** Subtracting $\lambda I$ touches only the diagonal ($I$ has entries off the diagonal all zero), so every zero below the diagonal of $A$ stays zero in $A - \lambda I$. The matrix is still triangular — that is the one fact that makes the whole computation trivial. <br> **Why the determinant factors so nicely.** For a triangular matrix the determinant is just the product down the diagonal (Day 8), no cofactor mess. So instead of an unpredictable degree-$n$ polynomial, $p_A(\lambda)$ arrives *pre-factored* as $(a_{11}-\lambda)\cdots(a_{nn}-\lambda)$. <br> **Reading off the roots.** A product is zero exactly when one factor is zero, and $(a_{ii}-\lambda)=0$ means $\lambda = a_{ii}$. By Theorem 10.1 the eigenvalues are the roots of $p_A$, so they are exactly the numbers sitting on the diagonal (with repeats counted by how often a value appears). This is why Exercises 1, 4, 5 could skip straight to the diagonal. <br> **What just happened:** for a triangular matrix the characteristic polynomial comes ready-factored down the diagonal, so its roots — the eigenvalues — are simply the diagonal entries. |

### Exercise 9 — do $A$ and $A^T$ share eigenvalues? eigenvectors?

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **9.** Yes, $A$ and $A^T$ always have the same eigenvalues, but their eigenvectors are generally *different*. For the eigenvalue claim: $p_{A^T}(\lambda) = \det(A^T - \lambda I) = \det\big((A-\lambda I)^T\big) = \det(A - \lambda I) = p_A(\lambda),$ using the fact that $\lambda I$ is symmetric (so $(A - \lambda I)^T = A^T - \lambda I$) and that a matrix and its transpose have the same determinant (Day 8). Since $A$ and $A^T$ have the *identical* characteristic polynomial, they have exactly the same roots, hence the same eigenvalues, by Theorem 10.1. For the concrete illustration, $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $3, 2$ with eigenvectors $(1,0)$ and $(1,-1)$ (Exercise 1). $A^T = \begin{pmatrix}3&0\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$: for $\lambda=3$, $A^T - 3I = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ gives $v_1 = v_2$, so eigenvector $(1,1)$ — *not* a multiple of $A$'s eigenvector $(1,0)$ for the same eigenvalue. For $\lambda=2$, $A^T-2I = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ gives $v_1 = 0$, so eigenvector $(0,1)$ — again different from $A$'s $(1,-1)$. So the trap is real: matching eigenvalues do **not** imply matching eigenvectors, because the proof above only ever compares *determinants* (which pin down $\lambda$), never the actual null spaces of $A - \lambda I$ versus $A^T - \lambda I$ (which pin down the eigenvectors, and depend on the specific rows/columns of $A$ versus $A^T$, generally different). | **Why eigenvalues must agree.** Eigenvalues depend on $A$ only through the *number* $\det(A-\lambda I)$, and transposing never changes a determinant (Day 8). The one subtlety is $(A-\lambda I)^T = A^T - \lambda I$: this works only because $\lambda I$ is symmetric, so transposing the shifted matrix simply shifts the transpose. The two characteristic polynomials are then literally the same function of $\lambda$, so they have identical roots. <br> **Why eigenvectors need not agree.** An eigenvalue is a single number the determinant pins down; an eigenvector is a whole *direction* — the null space of $A-\lambda I$. Transposing preserves the determinant but reshuffles which vectors get sent to zero (it swaps the roles of rows and columns). Equal determinants say nothing about equal null spaces, so the eigenvectors are free to move. <br> **What the example shows.** For $\lambda=3$, $A$ fixes the $x$-axis $(1,0)$ while $A^T$ fixes the diagonal $(1,1)$ — same stretch factor $3$, completely different invariant line. The determinant machinery that guaranteed the shared eigenvalue simply never looked at these directions. <br> **What just happened:** transpose leaves every determinant untouched, so the characteristic polynomial — and hence the eigenvalues — is identical; but it scrambles the null spaces, so the invariant directions (eigenvectors) generally change. |

### Exercise 10 — sum and product of the two eigenvalues of a $2\times 2$ matrix

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **10.** $p_A(\lambda) = \det\begin{pmatrix}a-\lambda & b \\ c & d-\lambda\end{pmatrix} = (a-\lambda)(d-\lambda) - bc = \lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad - bc) = \lambda^2 - \operatorname{trace}(A)\,\lambda + \det(A)$. By Theorem 10.1, $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are exactly the roots of $p_A$, so $p_A(\lambda) = (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2$. Comparing coefficients of these two expressions for the same polynomial $p_A(\lambda)$: the coefficient of $\lambda^1$ gives $-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2) = -\operatorname{trace}(A)$, i.e. $\lambda_1+\lambda_2 = \operatorname{trace}(A)$; the constant term gives $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det(A)$. | **The engine is "one polynomial, written two ways."** The proof computes $p_A(\lambda)$ twice: once from the entries $a,b,c,d$ (giving $\lambda^2 - (a{+}d)\lambda + (ad{-}bc)$), and once from the roots (giving $\lambda^2 - (\lambda_1{+}\lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2$). Both expand the *same* function, so their coefficients must match term by term — that matching is the whole argument. This is exactly Vieta's formulas for a monic quadratic. <br> **Why the coefficients are safe to compare.** Two polynomials that are equal as functions have equal coefficients (a degree-$2$ polynomial is pinned down by its three coefficients). Both forms here are monic (leading $\lambda^2$), so the $\lambda^2$ terms already agree; the remaining two coefficients then force $\lambda_1+\lambda_2 = a+d$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = ad-bc$. <br> **Why this is useful (and robust).** Trace and determinant are cheap to read straight off $A$, so for a $2\times2$ you get the eigenvalues' sum and product *without solving anything* — a fast sanity check on any eigenvalue computation. It also holds when the roots are complex or repeated, since the factorization $(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)$ is valid over $\mathbb{C}$ (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, Definition 10.2). <br> **What just happened:** writing the characteristic polynomial from the entries and from the roots and matching coefficients recovers Vieta's formulas — eigenvalues sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant. |
