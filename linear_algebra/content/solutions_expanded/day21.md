### Exercise 5 — nonzero singular values of $A$ and $A^T$ coincide

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Let $\lambda \neq 0$ be an eigenvalue of $A^TA$ with unit eigenvector $v$ ($A^TAv = \lambda v$). Consider $w = Av$. First, $w \neq 0$: if $w=0$ then $A^TAv = A^Tw = 0$, but $A^TAv=\lambda v\neq0$ (since $\lambda\neq0, v\neq0$) — contradiction. Now check $w$ is an eigenvector of $AA^T$ with the same eigenvalue: $AA^Tw = AA^T(Av) = A(A^TAv) = A(\lambda v) = \lambda(Av) = \lambda w.$ So every nonzero eigenvalue of $A^TA$ is also an eigenvalue of $AA^T$ (via $v \mapsto Av$). Running the identical argument with the roles of $A$ and $A^T$ swapped (i.e. starting from an eigenvector of $AA^T=(A^T)^TA^T$ and mapping via $A^T$) shows every nonzero eigenvalue of $AA^T$ is also an eigenvalue of $A^TA$. Moreover this correspondence is injective on eigenspaces (orthogonal eigenvectors of $A^TA$ map to orthogonal vectors — same computation as Theorem 21.1's Step 4 — so it preserves multiplicity). Hence the nonzero eigenvalues of $A^TA$ and $AA^T$ coincide as multisets, so $\sigma_i(A) = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^TA)} = \sqrt{\lambda_i(AA^T)} = \sigma_i(A^T)$ for the nonzero singular values. | **The idea in one line.** Singular values come from the eigenvalues of $A^TA$ (Theorem 21.1), so proving $\sigma(A)=\sigma(A^T)$ means proving $A^TA$ and $AA^T$ share their nonzero eigenvalues — and the bridge between them is the *same* map $v\mapsto Av$ that built $U$ from $V$. <br> **Why $w=Av$ is the right vector.** Recall Step 4 of Theorem 21.1 defined $u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i$ — pushing an eigenvector of $A^TA$ through $A$ to land in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$. Here $w=Av$ is that same push (un-normalized). The one-line computation $AA^T(Av)=A(A^TAv)=A(\lambda v)=\lambda(Av)$ just reassociates $A(A^TA)=(AA^T)A$ so the eigenvector-equation $A^TAv=\lambda v$ can be applied inside. So $A$ carries eigenvectors of $A^TA$ (living in the input space) to eigenvectors of $AA^T$ (living in the output space), keeping $\lambda$ unchanged. <br> **Why $\lambda\neq0$ matters.** The check $w\neq0$ is what needs it: if $Av=0$ then $A^TAv=0$, contradicting $A^TAv=\lambda v\neq0$. A zero eigenvalue *could* collapse to $w=0$ and carry no information — which is exactly why the claim is only about the **nonzero** singular values. <br> **Why the counts (multiplicities) match too.** A single matched pair of eigenvalues isn't enough; we need the same *number* of each. Orthonormal $v$'s map to orthogonal $Av$'s (Step 4's inner-product computation again), so an independent eigenspace of $A^TA$ maps to an equally-large independent eigenspace of $AA^T$ — no collapsing. Swapping $A\leftrightarrow A^T$ runs the reverse map, so the correspondence is a bijection and the multisets are equal. <br> **What just happened:** the nonzero stretch factors are a property of the $A$ / $A^T$ *pair*, not of which one you call "the matrix" — the very map that transports $A^TA$'s eigenvectors into $A$'s output space shows both matrices stretch by the same amounts. |

### Exercise 6 — $\operatorname{rank}(A)$ equals the number of nonzero singular values

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| *$\ker(A^TA) = \ker(A)$:* If $x \in \ker(A)$ (so $Ax=0$), then $A^TAx = A^T0 = 0$, so $x \in \ker(A^TA)$. Conversely, if $x\in\ker(A^TA)$ (so $A^TAx=0$), then $0 = x^T(A^TAx) = (Ax)^T(Ax) = \|Ax\|^2 \implies Ax = 0,$ so $x \in \ker(A)$. Both inclusions give $\ker(A^TA)=\ker(A)$, hence $\dim\ker(A^TA) = \dim\ker(A) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A)$ (rank-nullity, Day 4). Since $A^TA$ is symmetric, the Spectral Theorem gives it an orthonormal eigenbasis, and $\dim\ker(A^TA)$ equals exactly the number of zero eigenvalues among $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ (each zero eigenvalue contributes one dimension to the eigenspace, i.e. to the kernel, and distinct eigenspaces are independent). The number of zero $\lambda_i$'s is, by definition, $n - r$ (recall $r$ is the count of positive $\lambda_i$'s). So $n - \operatorname{rank}(A) = \dim\ker(A^TA) = n - r \implies \operatorname{rank}(A) = r,$ i.e. $\operatorname{rank}(A)$ equals the number of nonzero singular values. | **The three links in the chain.** We want $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$ (the number of positive $\sigma_i$). The proof chains three equalities of *kernels*: $\ker(A)=\ker(A^TA)$, then $\dim\ker(A^TA)=$ (number of zero $\lambda_i$) $=n-r$, then rank-nullity converts kernel dimension back to rank. <br> **Why $\ker(A^TA)=\ker(A)$ — the key trick reused.** The "$\subseteq$" direction $\ker(A)\subseteq\ker(A^TA)$ is free ($Ax=0\Rightarrow A^TAx=0$). The interesting direction uses Lemma 21.1's signature move: $x^T(A^TA)x=\|Ax\|^2$. If $A^TAx=0$ then this squared length is $0$, and only the zero vector has zero length, so $Ax=0$. Intuitively, $A^TA$ cannot kill any vector that $A$ itself doesn't already kill — squaring can't create new "invisible" directions. <br> **Why the Spectral Theorem is needed.** For a general matrix, "number of zero eigenvalues" and "kernel dimension" can differ (defective matrices). But $A^TA$ is symmetric, so Day 19 guarantees a full orthonormal eigenbasis — no defect — and then the geometric and algebraic multiplicities of $\lambda=0$ agree: $\dim\ker(A^TA)$ is exactly how many $\lambda_i$ are zero, which is $n-r$. <br> **Closing the loop.** Rank-nullity says $\dim\ker(A)=n-\operatorname{rank}(A)$. Setting the two kernel dimensions equal, $n-\operatorname{rank}(A)=n-r$, so $\operatorname{rank}(A)=r$. <br> **What just happened:** the nonzero singular values count exactly the independent directions $A$ stretches; the directions with $\sigma_i=0$ are precisely the ones $A$ collapses to zero — i.e. its kernel — so counting nonzero $\sigma_i$ is just counting rank. |

### Exercise 7 — $\sigma_1 = \max_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\|$ (largest singular value is the operator norm)

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $\|x\|=1$. Expand $x$ in the orthonormal eigenbasis $v_1,\dots,v_n$ from Theorem 21.1: $x = \sum_{i=1}^n c_iv_i$ with $\sum_i c_i^2 = \|x\|^2 = 1$ (Parseval, from orthonormality). Then $Ax = \sum_{i=1}^n c_iAv_i = \sum_{i=1}^r c_i\sigma_iu_i$ (using $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$ for $i\le r$, and $Av_i=0$ for $i>r$, shown in Theorem 21.1 Step 7). Since $u_1,\dots,u_r$ are orthonormal, $\|Ax\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r c_i^2\sigma_i^2 \le \sigma_1^2\sum_{i=1}^r c_i^2 \le \sigma_1^2\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2 = \sigma_1^2,$ using $\sigma_i \le \sigma_1$ for all $i\le r$ and $c_i^2\ge0$. So $\|Ax\|\le\sigma_1$ for every unit $x$. Equality is achieved at $x=v_1$ (then $c_1=1$, all other $c_i=0$, so $Ax = \sigma_1u_1$ and $\|Ax\| = \sigma_1\|u_1\|=\sigma_1$). Hence $\max_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\| = \sigma_1$. | **The picture.** In the $v$-basis, $A$ is diagonal: it takes $v_i$, stretches by $\sigma_i$, and points the result along $u_i$. So writing $x=\sum c_i v_i$ decomposes $x$ into how much it "asks for" each stretch direction, and the constraint $\sum c_i^2=1$ (a unit sphere in coordinates, via Parseval) is a fixed *budget* of that asking. <br> **Why the two orthonormal bases make it clean.** Because the $u_i$ are orthonormal, the output has no cross-terms: $\|Ax\|^2=\sum_{i\le r}c_i^2\sigma_i^2$ — a clean sum, not a mess of dot products. So $\|Ax\|^2$ is a *weighted average of the values $\sigma_i^2$*, with nonnegative weights $c_i^2$ that add to (at most) $1$. <br> **Why $\sigma_1$ wins.** A weighted average of numbers can never exceed the largest of them, so $\|Ax\|^2\le\sigma_1^2$: replacing every $\sigma_i$ by the biggest, $\sigma_1$, only inflates the sum. The two "$\le$" steps are exactly this (bounding each $\sigma_i\le\sigma_1$, then extending the sum from $r$ to $n$ terms, which is fine since the extra $c_i^2\ge0$). The bound is *achieved* by spending the entire budget on direction $v_1$ ($c_1=1$), which is why $\sigma_1$ is a genuine maximum, not just an upper bound. <br> **What just happened:** the most a matrix can stretch any unit vector is its largest singular value, and it does so precisely by feeding it the top right-singular vector $v_1$ — so $\sigma_1$ *is* the operator norm. |

### Exercise 8 — $\|A\|_F^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i^2$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| By definition, $\|A\|_F^2 = \sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^2$. The $(j,j)$ entry of $A^TA$ is $\sum_i A_{ij}^2$ (dotting column $j$ of $A$ with itself), so $\operatorname{trace}(A^TA) = \sum_j\sum_iA_{ij}^2 = \|A\|_F^2.$ Since $A^TA$ is symmetric, its trace equals the sum of its eigenvalues (true for any matrix via the characteristic polynomial, and particularly transparent here: in the eigenbasis $v_1,\dots,v_n$, $A^TA$ is similar to $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$, and trace is invariant under similarity). So $\|A\|_F^2 = \operatorname{trace}(A^TA) = \sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i = \sum_{i=1}^n\sigma_i^2.$ | **The bridge is the trace.** Two quantities that look unrelated — the entrywise sum of squares $\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^2$ and the stretch factors $\sigma_i$ — are connected because both equal $\operatorname{trace}(A^TA)$. <br> **First equality, unpacked.** The diagonal entry $(A^TA)_{jj}$ is column $j$ of $A$ dotted with itself, i.e. $\sum_i A_{ij}^2$. Summing over $j$ (the definition of trace) sweeps over *every* entry once, giving $\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^2=\|A\|_F^2$. So the Frobenius norm is secretly reading off the diagonal of $A^TA$. <br> **Second equality — why trace $=\sum\lambda_i$.** Trace is *similarity-invariant*: $\operatorname{trace}(P^{-1}MP)=\operatorname{trace}(M)$. Since $A^TA$ is symmetric, the Spectral Theorem diagonalizes it to $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ in the eigenbasis, whose trace is plainly $\sum\lambda_i$. And $\lambda_i=\sigma_i^2$ by the definition of singular values. <br> **The deeper point.** $\|A\|_F$ ignores coordinates — it's unchanged if you rotate the input or output frame. That is exactly why it can be expressed purely through the $\sigma_i$, which are themselves rotation-independent. Both are "total size" measured with no reference to a particular basis. <br> **What just happened:** adding up the squares of all of $A$'s entries gives the same number as adding up the squares of its stretch factors — the total energy of $A$, counted two equivalent ways. |

### Exercise 9 — why $A$ maps the unit sphere onto an ellipsoid with semi-axes $\sigma_i$ along $u_i$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Let $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$ with $\|x\|=1$, and set $y=Ax=U\Sigma V^Tx$. Write $w = V^Tx$. Since $V^T$ is orthogonal, it preserves norms (Day 17 Corollary), so $\|w\|=\|x\|=1$; moreover, as $x$ ranges over the *entire* unit sphere of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so does $w=V^Tx$ (because $V^T$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that preserves the sphere, with inverse $V$ also preserving the sphere). So the intermediate set $\{w : \|w\|=1\}$ is again exactly the unit sphere, just re-labeled by $V^T$. Next, $\Sigma$ acts on $w=(w_1,\dots,w_n)$ by $(\Sigma w)_i = \sigma_iw_i$ for $i=1,\dots,\min(m,n)$ (and drops/pads coordinates if $m\neq n$) — i.e. it stretches (or shrinks) the $i$-th coordinate axis by the factor $\sigma_i$ independently for each $i$. The image of the unit sphere $\{w:\|w\|=1\}$ under an independent per-axis stretch is, by definition, an **axis-aligned ellipsoid** with semi-axis lengths exactly the stretch factors $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{\min(m,n)}$ (in the standard coordinate directions $e_1,\dots,e_{\min(m,n)}$ of $\mathbb{R}^m$); directions $i$ with $\sigma_i=0$ collapse that axis to a point, consistent with the ellipsoid degenerating in exactly the directions killed by $A$ (Exercise 6: this happens along $\operatorname{rank}(A)$-many nonzero axes and $n-\operatorname{rank}(A)$ collapsed ones). Finally, $y = U(\Sigma w)$. Since $U$ is orthogonal, it preserves all lengths and angles (Day 17, Theorem 17.1 and Corollary) — it is a rigid rotation/reflection of $\mathbb{R}^m$. Applying a rigid motion to an ellipsoid produces another ellipsoid with the *same* semi-axis lengths, merely reoriented: the standard axis directions $e_1,\dots,e_m$ become $u_1,\dots,u_m$. So the final image $\{Ax : \|x\|=1\}$ is an ellipsoid in $\mathbb{R}^m$ with semi-axis lengths $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{\min(m,n)}$, oriented along the directions $u_1,\dots,u_{\min(m,n)}$ — exactly the claim. | **The choreography.** $A=U\Sigma V^T$ is read right-to-left as a three-move dance done to the input sphere: **rotate** ($V^T$), **stretch each axis** ($\Sigma$), **rotate** ($U$). The proof simply tracks the sphere through each move. <br> **Move 1 leaves no trace — and that's the point.** A rotation/reflection of a *sphere* gives back the same sphere (a sphere looks identical from every angle). So $V^T$ contributes nothing to the final shape; it only decides which input direction gets labeled "axis 1," etc. This is why the sphere is the perfect test object: it's blind to rotation, so only the stretch $\Sigma$ can leave a visible mark. <br> **Move 2 is where the ellipsoid is born.** Stretching coordinate $i$ by $\sigma_i$ turns the equation $\sum w_i^2=1$ into $\sum (y_i/\sigma_i)^2=1$ — the definition of an axis-aligned ellipsoid with radii $\sigma_i$. Where $\sigma_i=0$, that axis is crushed to a point (the degenerate directions from Exercise 6). <br> **Move 3 just carries it into place.** $U$ is rigid, so it can't change any of the ellipsoid's radii — it only swings the whole shape so that its axes, which were the coordinate directions $e_i$, now point along $u_i$. Lengths in, lengths out: semi-axes stay $\sigma_i$. <br> **What just happened:** every matrix is geometrically "rotate, stretch along axes, rotate," so it always sends a sphere to an ellipsoid whose radii are the singular values $\sigma_i$ and whose axes point along the left singular vectors $u_i$ — the SVD *is* the shape of that transformation. |

### Exercise 10 — positive definite $\Rightarrow$ singular values $=$ eigenvalues and $U=V$ (True)

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| True. Since $A$ is symmetric, the Spectral Theorem gives $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$; since $A$ is additionally positive **definite** (Day 20), all $\lambda_i > 0$ (strictly, unlike the merely semidefinite case). Then, using $A^T=A$, $A^TA = A\cdot A = (Q\Lambda Q^T)(Q\Lambda Q^T) = Q\Lambda(Q^TQ)\Lambda Q^T = Q\Lambda^2Q^T$ (using $Q^TQ=I$). This is a spectral decomposition of $A^TA$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_i^2$ and the *same* eigenvectors (columns of $Q$) as $A$ itself. So the singular values are $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i^2} = \lambda_i$ (using $\lambda_i>0$, so the positive square root recovers $\lambda_i$ exactly, not $\vert \lambda_i\vert $ trivially but genuinely the same positive number). Finally, $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ is already of the form $U\Sigma V^T$ with $U=Q$, $\Sigma=\Lambda$, $V=Q$ — a valid SVD with $U=V$. (This also explains, in hindsight, why Exercise 3's matrix — symmetric but only positive *semi*definite, with a zero eigenvalue — needed $A^TA=A^2$ computed from scratch rather than quoting this shortcut: the shortcut's conclusion "$\sigma_i=\lambda_i$" still happens to hold there too since all eigenvalues of that particular $A$ are non-negative, but the "$U=V$" part needs $u_i=v_i$ to be forced by $A$, which fails exactly at the zero eigenvalue, matching the free-choice freedom found in Exercise 3.) | **The intuition.** SVD's whole reason for needing *two* bases $U$ and $V$ is that a general matrix doesn't act on a single space nicely. But a symmetric positive-definite matrix already *does* — the Spectral Theorem writes it as "rotate, stretch, rotate-back" with the **same** rotation $Q$ on both ends. So for such an $A$, eigendecomposition and SVD ought to be one and the same, and the proof confirms it. <br> **Why $A^TA=Q\Lambda^2 Q^T$.** With $A$ symmetric, $A^TA=A^2$. Squaring $Q\Lambda Q^T$ lets the inner $Q^TQ=I$ cancel, leaving $Q\Lambda^2 Q^T$. So $A^TA$ has the *same* eigenvectors (columns of $Q$) as $A$, with eigenvalues squared — meaning $V$ (from $A^TA$'s eigenvectors) can be taken to be $Q$. <br> **Why positive-definiteness is the crucial hypothesis.** $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i^2}=\vert \lambda_i\vert $ always for a symmetric matrix. The strict positivity $\lambda_i>0$ is what upgrades $\vert \lambda_i\vert $ to $\lambda_i$ (no sign flips), so $\Sigma=\Lambda$ exactly — and, crucially, it forces every $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i=\lambda_i q_i/\lambda_i=q_i$, so $U=Q=V$ is *forced*, not merely allowed. If some $\lambda_i$ were negative, $u_i$ would flip to $-q_i$ and $U\neq V$; if some $\lambda_i=0$ (semidefinite), $u_i$ becomes a free choice (Exercise 3), again breaking $U=V$. <br> **What just happened:** positive-definiteness is exactly the condition under which SVD and eigendecomposition collapse into the identical factorization — same axes on both sides ($U=V=Q$), and stretch factors that are literally the eigenvalues, no absolute values needed. |
