### Exercise 9 — Polarization identity: $\langle u,v\rangle = \tfrac{\|u+v\|^2-\|u-v\|^2}{4}$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Expand both squared norms on the right using bilinearity and symmetry, exactly as in the proofs of Theorems 14.2/14.3: <br> $\|u+v\|^2 = \langle u,u\rangle + 2\langle u,v\rangle + \langle v,v\rangle = \|u\|^2+2\langle u,v\rangle+\|v\|^2,$ <br> $\|u-v\|^2 = \langle u,u\rangle - 2\langle u,v\rangle + \langle v,v\rangle = \|u\|^2-2\langle u,v\rangle+\|v\|^2,$ <br> Subtracting the second from the first, the $\|u\|^2$ and $\|v\|^2$ terms cancel exactly: <br> $\|u+v\|^2 - \|u-v\|^2 = 4\langle u,v\rangle.$ <br> Dividing both sides by $4$: <br> $\langle u,v\rangle = \frac{\|u+v\|^2-\|u-v\|^2}{4}. \qquad \blacksquare$ <br> (Numeric sanity check with $u=(2,3,-1)$, $v=(1,-2,4)$: $\langle u,v\rangle = 2-6-4=-8$, and $\|u+v\|^2-\|u-v\|^2 = \|(3,1,3)\|^2-\|(1,5,-5)\|^2 = 19-51=-32$, and $-32/4=-8$ — matches, confirmed numerically.) | **Intuition — where the cross term lives.** Squaring $u+v$ produces three pieces: a "pure $u$" piece $\|u\|^2$, a "pure $v$" piece $\|v\|^2$, and a cross term $2\langle u,v\rangle$ that carries *all* the angle information. Squaring $u-v$ produces the same two pure pieces but the cross term flips sign to $-2\langle u,v\rangle$. So subtracting kills everything you don't want (the pure pieces are identical in both) and *doubles* the one thing you do want. <br> **Why the key step is legal.** The expansion $\langle u\pm v,\ u\pm v\rangle = \langle u,u\rangle \pm 2\langle u,v\rangle + \langle v,v\rangle$ uses only bilinearity (distribute the inner product over the sum in each slot) and symmetry $\langle u,v\rangle=\langle v,u\rangle$ (so the two off-diagonal terms merge into one $2\langle u,v\rangle$) — both are inner-product axioms, and the "$2$" appears *because* symmetry lets the two mixed terms combine. Nothing here uses coordinates, so the identity holds in any real inner product space. <br> **Why it matters.** The norm $\|\cdot\|$ seems to throw away directional information — it returns a single number. This identity shows it doesn't: the full inner product (hence every angle) can be reconstructed from norms of sums and differences alone. That is the precise sense in which "the norm determines the geometry." <br> **What just happened:** adding and subtracting the two squared-norm expansions cancels the length terms and isolates $4\langle u,v\rangle$, so the inner product is recoverable from lengths alone. |

### Exercise 10 — $W_1 \subseteq W_2 \implies W_2^\perp \subseteq W_1^\perp$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Let $x \in W_2^\perp$, i.e. $\langle x,w\rangle = 0$ for every $w \in W_2$. Since $W_1 \subseteq W_2$, every $w \in W_1$ is also in $W_2$, so in particular $\langle x,w\rangle = 0$ holds for every $w \in W_1$ as well. This is exactly the defining condition for $x \in W_1^\perp$. Since $x \in W_2^\perp$ was arbitrary, $W_2^\perp \subseteq W_1^\perp$. $\blacksquare$ (Intuition: the *bigger* the subspace $W$, the *more* orthogonality conditions a vector in $W^\perp$ must satisfy, so the *smaller* $W^\perp$ can be — orthogonal complementation reverses inclusions, mirroring how $(W^\perp)^\perp = W$ from Day 15 Exercise 6 is consistent with applying this inclusion-reversal twice.) | **Intuition — being perpendicular to more is harder.** Membership in $W^\perp$ is a *checklist*: $x$ must be orthogonal to every vector in $W$. Enlarging $W$ from $W_1$ to $W_2$ only adds items to that checklist — it never removes any. A vector that already passes the *longer* checklist ($x\in W_2^\perp$) automatically passes the *shorter* one ($x\in W_1^\perp$), because the shorter list is a sub-list of the longer. That single observation is the whole proof; the argument never needs to build $x$ or use coordinates. <br> **Why the logic is airtight.** "$W_1\subseteq W_2$" means literally that each $w\in W_1$ is also a $w\in W_2$, so a condition quantified over all of $W_2$ ("$\langle x,w\rangle=0$ for all $w\in W_2$") is *stronger than* the same condition quantified over the subset $W_1$. Passing the stronger test implies passing the weaker one — no extra machinery required. <br> **Sanity check against a known fact.** Order-reversal is exactly what makes $(W^\perp)^\perp = W$ (Day 15 Ex. 6) consistent: applying an order-reversing operation twice restores the original order, just as negating twice restores a number's sign. <br> **What just happened:** a bigger subspace imposes a longer orthogonality checklist, so its complement is smaller — perp reverses inclusions. |

### Exercise 11 — $\operatorname{proj}_W$ is idempotent

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Let $e_1,\dots,e_k$ be an orthonormal basis of $W$, and write $p = \operatorname{proj}_W(v) = \sum_{i=1}^k \langle v,e_i\rangle e_i$ (Definition 16.1). Apply Definition 16.1 again, this time to $p$: <br> $\operatorname{proj}_W(p) = \sum_{j=1}^k \langle p,e_j\rangle e_j.$ <br> Compute $\langle p,e_j\rangle$ using bilinearity and orthonormality ($\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$): <br> $\langle p,e_j\rangle = \Big\langle \sum_{i=1}^k \langle v,e_i\rangle e_i,\ e_j\Big\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^k \langle v,e_i\rangle\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = \langle v,e_j\rangle,$ <br> since only the $i=j$ term survives. Substituting back: <br> $\operatorname{proj}_W(p) = \sum_{j=1}^k \langle v,e_j\rangle e_j = p.$ <br> So $\operatorname{proj}_W(\operatorname{proj}_W(v)) = \operatorname{proj}_W(v)$ for every $v \in V$: $\operatorname{proj}_W$ is idempotent. $\blacksquare$ (This is the algebraic form of "projecting an already-projected vector does nothing," and it's a special case of Day 16 Exercise 7 — $\operatorname{proj}_W(w)=w$ for $w \in W$ — applied to $w = p \in W$; the derivation here just redoes that argument from scratch for this specific $p$.) | **Intuition — the coordinates are already correct.** In the orthonormal basis $e_1,\dots,e_k$, the projection $p=\operatorname{proj}_W(v)$ is built by reading off $v$'s coordinate along each axis, namely $\langle v,e_j\rangle$. To project $p$ *again* you re-read those same coordinates, but now from $p$. The point of the computation is that $p$'s coordinate along $e_j$ is *identical* to $v$'s: reading a coordinate a second time cannot change it. So the second projection returns the very same combination. <br> **Why the key step works.** The collapse $\langle p,e_j\rangle=\langle v,e_j\rangle$ is where orthonormality earns its keep: $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$ means every term in the sum vanishes except $i=j$, which contributes $\langle v,e_j\rangle\cdot 1$. Without orthonormality the off-axis terms $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle$ would leak in and $p$ would not reproduce $v$'s coordinates. Bilinearity is what lets the constant $\langle v,e_i\rangle$ pull out of the inner product. <br> **The geometric one-liner.** $p$ already lives inside $W$, and the closest point of $W$ to a point already in $W$ is that point itself — so projecting it moves nothing. The algebra above is just that fact ($\operatorname{proj}_W(w)=w$ for $w\in W$, Day 16 Ex. 7) re-derived for $w=p$. <br> **What just happened:** projecting reads off orthonormal coordinates, and $p$ already has $v$'s coordinates, so projecting $p$ returns $p$ unchanged. |

### Exercise 12 — Real eigenvalue of an orthogonal matrix is $\pm 1$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Since $Q$ is orthogonal, the Corollary to Theorem 17.1 gives $\|Qx\| = \|x\|$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Apply this with $x=v$: $\|Qv\| = \|v\|$. But $v$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$, so $Qv = \lambda v$, hence <br> $\|Qv\| = \|\lambda v\| = \vert \lambda\vert \,\|v\|.$ <br> Combining, $\vert \lambda\vert \,\|v\| = \|v\|$. Since $v$ is an eigenvector, $v \neq 0$, so $\|v\| \neq 0$ (positive-definiteness), and we can divide both sides by $\|v\|$: <br> $\vert \lambda\vert  = 1 \implies \lambda = \pm 1. \qquad \blacksquare$ <br> (This is exactly why rotation matrices, which are orthogonal, have only complex eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\theta}$ when $\theta \neq 0,\pi$ — a rotation in the plane has *no* real eigenvector at all unless it's the identity or a $180°$ rotation, precisely the two cases $\lambda=1,-1$ allowed here.) | **Intuition — an isometry cannot stretch.** An orthogonal matrix is a rigid motion: it preserves every length, $\|Qx\|=\|x\|$. An eigenvector is a direction that $Q$ merely scales, by the factor $\lambda$. But scaling by $\lambda$ multiplies length by $\vert \lambda\vert $, and a length-preserving map is allowed a length multiplier of exactly $1$. So the *only* scale factors a rigid motion can apply along any real invariant direction are those with $\vert \lambda\vert =1$ — and among *real* numbers that leaves just $+1$ (fixed) and $-1$ (flipped). <br> **Why each step is legal.** $\|Qv\|=\|v\|$ is the length-preservation corollary of Theorem 17.1. $\|\lambda v\|=\vert \lambda\vert \,\|v\|$ is absolute homogeneity of the norm — a norm axiom, and the reason it's $\vert \lambda\vert $ and not $\lambda$ is that lengths are nonnegative. Dividing by $\|v\|$ is valid precisely because eigenvectors are nonzero and positive-definiteness forces $\|v\|>0$; you could not perform this cancellation if $v$ were allowed to be $0$. <br> **Why "real" is essential.** The argument bounds $\vert \lambda\vert =1$; over the reals that forces $\lambda=\pm1$, but over the complex numbers it only forces $\lambda$ onto the unit circle. That is exactly why a genuine rotation, though orthogonal, has complex eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\theta}$ and *no* real eigenvector unless $\theta=0$ (identity, $\lambda=1$) or $\theta=\pi$ ($180°$ flip, $\lambda=-1$). <br> **What just happened:** a length-preserving map can only rescale a real direction by a factor of size $1$, so a real eigenvalue must be $\pm 1$. |

### Exercise 14 — Why Gram-Schmidt needs linear independence

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Gram-Schmidt builds $u_k = v_k - \sum_{i<k} \frac{\langle v_k,u_i\rangle}{\langle u_i,u_i\rangle}u_i$, and this subtracted sum is exactly $\operatorname{proj}_{\operatorname{span}\{u_1,\dots,u_{k-1}\}}(v_k)$, which by the span-equality property equals $\operatorname{proj}_{\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_{k-1}\}}(v_k)$. If $v_3 \in \operatorname{span}\{v_1,v_2\}$ (a dependent set), then $v_3$ is already entirely "explained" by $v_1,v_2$, so by Day 16 Exercise 7 its projection onto that span is $v_3$ itself — meaning $u_3 = v_3 - \operatorname{proj}(v_3) = v_3 - v_3 = 0$ exactly. Normalizing then requires dividing by $\|u_3\|=0$, which is undefined, so the process breaks down at that step rather than silently producing a wrong-but-valid answer. This is precisely the contradiction Theorem 15.2's proof of part (a) rules out by assuming the $v_i$ are linearly independent in the first place. | **Intuition — each step keeps only the genuinely new direction.** Gram-Schmidt's move is: take $v_k$, subtract off the part of it that already lies in the space spanned by the earlier vectors, and keep the leftover $u_k$ — the truly *new* direction $v_k$ contributes. If $v_k$ contributes nothing new, there is no leftover to keep, and that is precisely the failure mode. <br> **Tracing the breakdown step by step.** If $v_3\in\operatorname{span}\{v_1,v_2\}$, then $v_3$ is *entirely* inside the earlier span, so the "part already explained" is all of $v_3$. Formally its projection onto that span is $v_3$ itself (a vector in $W$ projects to itself, Day 16 Ex. 7), so $u_3 = v_3 - v_3 = 0$. The next instruction is to normalize: $e_3 = u_3/\|u_3\| = 0/0$ — division by zero, undefined. <br> **Why this is a feature, not a bug.** The algorithm does not quietly hand back a plausible-but-wrong "orthonormal" vector; it halts at an impossible division, flagging that the input was dependent. This is the same collapse Theorem 15.2 forecloses by *assuming* independence up front — independence is exactly the hypothesis that guarantees every $u_k\neq 0$, so normalization is always legal. The span-equality property is what licenses swapping $\operatorname{span}\{u_1,\dots,u_{k-1}\}$ for $\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_{k-1}\}$ mid-argument. <br> **What just happened:** a dependent vector has no new direction to contribute, so its Gram-Schmidt leftover is the zero vector, and normalizing it demands an illegal $0/0$ — that is exactly why independence is required. |

### Exercise 15 — $\|b\|^2 = \|A\hat x\|^2 + \|b - A\hat x\|^2$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| From the proof of Theorem 16.2, a vector $\hat x$ satisfying the normal equations $A^TA\hat x = A^Tb$ satisfies $A^T(b-A\hat x) = 0$, i.e. $b - A\hat x \in N(A^T) = C(A)^\perp$. Also $A\hat x \in C(A)$ trivially (it's $A$ applied to some vector). Since $A\hat x \in C(A)$ and $b-A\hat x \in C(A)^\perp$, these two vectors are orthogonal by definition of orthogonal complement: $\langle A\hat x,\ b-A\hat x\rangle = 0$. <br> Now write $b = A\hat x + (b - A\hat x)$ and apply Lemma 16.1 (Pythagorean theorem for orthogonal vectors) with $a = A\hat x$, $b_{\text{vec}} = b - A\hat x$: <br> $\|b\|^2 = \|A\hat x + (b-A\hat x)\|^2 = \|A\hat x\|^2 + \|b-A\hat x\|^2. \qquad \blacksquare$ <br> (Numeric check using Problem 7's data: $y=(1,2,4,7)$, $\hat x = (2,-1.5)$, $A\hat x = (0.5,2.5,4.5,6.5)$, residual $(0.5,-0.5,-0.5,0.5)$. $\|y\|^2 = 1+4+16+49=70$. $\|A\hat x\|^2+\|\text{residual}\|^2 = (0.25+6.25+20.25+42.25) + (0.25+0.25+0.25+0.25) = 69+1=70$ — matches exactly, confirmed numerically, and $\langle A\hat x,\text{residual}\rangle$ is $0$ to floating-point precision as the proof predicts.)  | **Intuition — least squares is a right triangle.** Least squares splits the target $b$ into two perpendicular pieces: the fitted part $A\hat x$ (the shadow of $b$ on the column space $C(A)$) and the residual $b-A\hat x$ (what's left over, sticking straight out of $C(A)$). Those two legs meet at a right angle, so $b$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle and the identity is just the Pythagorean theorem $\text{hyp}^2 = \text{leg}^2 + \text{leg}^2$. <br> **Why the right angle is guaranteed.** The normal equations $A^TA\hat x=A^Tb$ rearrange to $A^T(b-A\hat x)=0$, which says the residual is killed by $A^T$ — i.e. it lives in $N(A^T)=C(A)^\perp$. Meanwhile $A\hat x$ is a combination of $A$'s columns, so it lives in $C(A)$. A vector in a space and a vector in that space's orthogonal complement are perpendicular *by definition*, giving $\langle A\hat x,\ b-A\hat x\rangle=0$ for free — no computation needed. This is the entire content of "the normal equations make the residual orthogonal to the fit." <br> **Why the cross term vanishes.** Expanding $\|A\hat x+(b-A\hat x)\|^2$ by bilinearity produces the two squared norms plus $2\langle A\hat x,\ b-A\hat x\rangle$; orthogonality zeroes that middle term (Lemma 16.1), leaving a clean sum of squares. <br> **What just happened:** the fit and the residual are perpendicular legs of a right triangle with $b$ as hypotenuse, so their squared lengths add up to $\|b\|^2$. |
