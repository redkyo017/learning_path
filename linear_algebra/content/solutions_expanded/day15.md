### Exercise 6 — $(W^\perp)^\perp = W$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **6.** *($W \subseteq (W^\perp)^\perp$):* let $w \in W$. For every $u \in W^\perp$, by definition of $W^\perp$, $\langle u, w\rangle = 0$ (since $w \in W$), and by symmetry $\langle w, u\rangle = 0$ too. Since this holds for every $u \in W^\perp$, $w$ satisfies the defining condition of $(W^\perp)^\perp$, i.e. $w \in (W^\perp)^\perp$. So $W \subseteq (W^\perp)^\perp$.<br><br>*(Dimension count):* Applying Theorem 15.1 to $W$: $\dim V = \dim W + \dim W^\perp$. Applying Theorem 15.1 to the subspace $W^\perp$ in place of $W$: $\dim V = \dim W^\perp + \dim (W^\perp)^\perp$. The left sides are equal, so $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = \dim W^\perp + \dim(W^\perp)^\perp$, hence $\dim W = \dim (W^\perp)^\perp$.<br><br>Since $W \subseteq (W^\perp)^\perp$ and these two subspaces of the finite-dimensional space $V$ have the same (finite) dimension, they are equal: if $W$ were a proper subset of $(W^\perp)^\perp$, any basis of $W$ could be extended to a strictly larger linearly independent set inside $(W^\perp)^\perp$, forcing $\dim(W^\perp)^\perp > \dim W$, contradicting what we just showed. So $W = (W^\perp)^\perp$. $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition — "perp" is a flip, and flipping twice returns you home.** Think of $R^3$: if $W$ is a plane through the origin, $W^\perp$ is the line normal to it; the things perpendicular to *that line* are exactly the original plane again. Taking the orthogonal complement twice should give back $W$ — the proof just makes "should" rigorous. <br> **Why the easy inclusion is almost a tautology.** $(W^\perp)^\perp$ means "everything orthogonal to everything in $W^\perp$." But every $w \in W$ is *by construction* orthogonal to every vector in $W^\perp$ (that is what $W^\perp$ collects, Definition 15.1). Symmetry $\langle u,w\rangle = \langle w,u\rangle$ lets you read that orthogonality from either side, so $w$ automatically qualifies: $W \subseteq (W^\perp)^\perp$ costs nothing. <br> **Why the hard part is ruling out "too big."** The real worry is that $(W^\perp)^\perp$ might be *strictly larger* than $W$. Dimensions close that door. Theorem 15.1 says complementing subtracts from $\dim V$: $\dim W^\perp = \dim V - \dim W$. Apply the same subtraction to $W^\perp$ and you land back where you started, $\dim(W^\perp)^\perp = \dim V - \dim W^\perp = \dim W$. A subspace contained in another of the *same finite dimension* cannot be smaller — there is no room to grow past it — so containment upgrades to equality. <br> **What just happened:** complementing is an involution — do it twice and the $\dim V - (\cdot)$ subtractions cancel, so you return to exactly $W$. |

### Exercise 7 — is $W \cap W^\perp = \{0\}$ always, and what does positive-definiteness buy?

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **7.** Yes, always. Let $x \in W \cap W^\perp$. Since $x \in W^\perp$, $x$ is orthogonal to every vector of $W$; since also $x \in W$, in particular $x$ is orthogonal to itself: $\langle x,x\rangle = 0$. By positive-definiteness of the inner product ($\langle x,x\rangle = 0 \implies x=0$), $x = 0$. Since $0$ is trivially in both $W$ and $W^\perp$, $W \cap W^\perp = \{0\}$ for every subspace $W$ — this is forced by positive-definiteness alone, not something that needs re-checking case by case (it's exactly the fact used to prove uniqueness in Theorem 15.1). The argument uses *only* positive-definiteness ($\langle x,x\rangle=0 \implies x=0$), nothing else about the specific inner product; it would **not** work for a symmetric bilinear form that isn't positive-definite, since such a form can have nonzero "self-orthogonal" (null) vectors with $\langle x,x\rangle=0$ but $x \neq 0$ — this is exactly what happens with the Minkowski form in special relativity, where light-like vectors are self-orthogonal and nonzero. | **Intuition — the only vector perpendicular to itself is the zero vector.** Living in both $W$ and $W^\perp$ means $x$ must be orthogonal to everything in $W$ *including $x$ itself*, so $\langle x,x\rangle = 0$. In a genuine geometry $\langle x,x\rangle = \|x\|^2$ is a squared length — and the only thing with zero length is the zero vector. That is the whole proof in one image. <br> **Where positive-definiteness is doing the work.** "$\langle x,x\rangle = 0 \implies x = 0$" is *precisely* the positive-definiteness axiom (from the Day 15 setup: $\langle v,v\rangle \ge 0$, with equality iff $v=0$). Nothing else about the specific form is used — not the dimension of $W$, not which inner product. So the conclusion is uniform across all subspaces; it never needs re-checking. <br> **Why it can fail without it.** Drop positive-definiteness and keep only symmetry + bilinearity, and a form can have nonzero *null* vectors with $\langle x,x\rangle = 0$. Then $x$ can sit in $W \cap W^\perp$ while being nonzero, and the intersection is no longer trivial. The Minkowski form of relativity is the standard example: a light-like vector is nonzero yet orthogonal to itself, so its "self-perpendicularity" carries no information about being zero. <br> **What just happened:** being perpendicular to yourself forces zero squared length, and positive-definiteness is exactly the promise that zero length means the zero vector — remove that promise and the trap springs. |

### Exercise 8 — any orthonormal set is linearly independent

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** Suppose $\{e_1,\dots,e_k\}$ is orthonormal ($\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$) and $c_1e_1+\cdots+c_ke_k = 0$. Fix any $j \in \{1,\dots,k\}$ and take the inner product of both sides with $e_j$:<br>$0 = \langle 0, e_j\rangle = \Big\langle \sum_{i=1}^k c_ie_i,\ e_j\Big\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i \langle e_i,e_j\rangle = c_j\langle e_j,e_j\rangle = c_j \cdot 1 = c_j,$<br>using bilinearity and that every term with $i \ne j$ vanishes while the $i=j$ term is $c_j \cdot 1$. Since $j$ was arbitrary, $c_j = 0$ for every $j = 1,\dots,k$: the only linear combination of $e_1,\dots,e_k$ equal to $0$ is the trivial one, so $\{e_1,\dots,e_k\}$ is linearly independent. $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition — each $e_j$ is a probe that reads off its own coefficient.** Dotting a combination $\sum_i c_i e_i$ with $e_j$ acts like a measuring instrument tuned to the $e_j$-axis: orthogonality ($\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = 0$ for $i \ne j$, Definition 15.2) silences every *other* direction, and unit length ($\langle e_j,e_j\rangle = 1$) means the reading isn't rescaled — so what comes back is exactly $c_j$, undistorted. <br> **Why that kills dependence.** Linear independence just says: the only way to build the zero vector is with all coefficients zero. Here the combination *is* the zero vector, so every probe reports $0$: $c_j = \langle 0, e_j\rangle = 0$. Running the probe for each $j$ from $1$ to $k$ zeroes out every coefficient at once. <br> **Where each axiom enters.** Bilinearity is what lets you push $\langle \cdot, e_j\rangle$ through the sum and pull the scalars $c_i$ out; the Kronecker-delta orthonormality condition $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$ is what collapses the whole sum to the single term $c_j$. No positive-definiteness needed — orthonormality alone suffices. <br> **What just happened:** perpendicular unit vectors don't overlap, so each one carries an independent piece of information; dotting a zero combination against each recovers its coefficient as $0$, leaving no room for a nontrivial dependence. |

### Exercise 9 — why every intermediate span is preserved, not just the final one

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **9.** Each $u_k$ is built only from $v_k$ and $u_1,\dots,u_{k-1}$ — the formula never references $v_{k+1},\dots,v_n$. So Gram-Schmidt doesn't just happen to produce a final orthonormal basis for the whole span; it produces a whole nested chain of orthonormal bases, one for every intermediate subspace $\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}$, "for free," in one pass. This matters because it means the $k$-th orthonormal vector depends only on the first $k$ original vectors, never on later ones — exactly the property needed for QR decomposition (Day 17), where the $k$-th column of $Q$ must be computable from only the first $k$ columns of the original matrix. | **Intuition — the process only ever looks backward.** The defining recursion $u_k = v_k - \sum_{i<k}\frac{\langle v_k,u_i\rangle}{\langle u_i,u_i\rangle}u_i$ (Theorem 15.2) reaches *back* to $u_1,\dots,u_{k-1}$ and never *forward* to $v_{k+1},\dots,v_n$. So the input needed to produce output $k$ is exactly the first $k$ originals — a "causal," triangular dependence. <br> **Why that gives a nested chain, not just one basis.** Part (c) of Theorem 15.2 is proved *at every step $k$*, not only at $k=n$: $\operatorname{span}\{u_1,\dots,u_k\} = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}$ for each $k$. Since each step adds one vector without disturbing the earlier outputs, you get a growing tower of orthonormal bases — one per prefix — all from a single left-to-right sweep, with no rework. <br> **Why the "prefix-only" property is the useful part.** In QR (Day 17) the columns of $Q$ are these $e_k$, and $A = QR$ forces $R$ to be upper-triangular precisely because column $k$ of $Q$ is expressible from — and expresses — only the first $k$ columns of $A$. If $e_k$ secretly depended on a later $v$, that triangular structure would break. The intermediate-span guarantee is exactly what makes the factorization well-defined column by column. <br> **What just happened:** because the formula only reaches backward, Gram-Schmidt orthonormalizes every prefix simultaneously, and that triangular "each output uses only earlier inputs" structure is the backbone QR is built on. |
