### Exercise 12 — Eigenvalue shift: $\lambda - c$ is an eigenvalue of $A - cI$ (same eigenvector)

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Since $v$ is an eigenvector of $A$ for $\lambda$: $Av=\lambda v$, $v \neq 0$. Compute directly: $(A-cI)v = Av - cv = \lambda v - cv = (\lambda - c)v.$ Since $v \neq 0$, this is exactly the statement that $v$ is an eigenvector of $A - cI$ with eigenvalue $\lambda - c$. $\blacksquare$ | **Step 1 — What the hypothesis gives.** By the definition of eigenvector (Day 10), $Av=\lambda v$ and $v\neq 0$. That $v\neq 0$ is not a technicality: the zero vector satisfies $Ax=\lambda x$ for *every* $\lambda$, so it is barred from ever being an eigenvector — and we need it barred to conclude anything about $A-cI$. <br> **Step 2 — Apply $A-cI$ to $v$.** Matrix-times-vector distributes over subtraction, so $(A-cI)v = Av - (cI)v$. The identity does nothing, $Iv=v$, hence $(cI)v = cv$. Substituting $Av=\lambda v$: $(A-cI)v = \lambda v - cv = (\lambda-c)v$. <br> **Step 3 — Read off the conclusion.** Since $v\neq 0$ (Step 1) and $(A-cI)v = (\lambda-c)v$, the vector $v$ meets the definition of an eigenvector of $A-cI$ with eigenvalue $\lambda-c$. <br> **Intuition.** Subtracting $cI$ is *pure scaling that touches every direction equally* — it stretches nothing relative to anything else. So it cannot rotate or reshape eigenvectors; it can only slide each eigenvalue down by the same constant $c$. The eigenvector $v$ never notices the shift, because along $v$ the matrix $A$ already acts like the scalar $\lambda$, and $A-cI$ then acts like the scalar $\lambda-c$. <br> **What just happened:** subtracting $cI$ from $A$ drops every eigenvalue by $c$ while leaving the eigenvectors exactly where they were. |

### Exercise 14 — Powers of a diagonalizable matrix drive $x_n \to av_1$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| By hypothesis $Av_1 = \lambda_1v_1 = v_1$ (since $\lambda_1=1$), so $A^nv_1 = v_1$ for every $n \ge 0$ (apply $A$ repeatedly: each application leaves $v_1$ unchanged). Also $Av_2=\lambda_2v_2=cv_2$, so by Theorem 12.1's argument applied to the single eigenvector $v_2$, $A^nv_2 = c^nv_2$. By linearity of $A^n$, $x_n = A^nx_0 = A^n(av_1+bv_2) = aA^nv_1 + bA^nv_2 = av_1 + bc^nv_2.$ Since $\vert c\vert <1$, $c^n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ (standard scalar limit), so $bc^n \to 0$, and hence $bc^nv_2 \to 0$ (the zero vector — a fixed vector $v_2$ scaled by a scalar going to $0$ goes to $0$). Therefore $x_n = av_1 + bc^nv_2 \longrightarrow av_1 + 0 = av_1,$ regardless of the value of $b$ (as long as $a \neq 0$ keeps the limit nonzero and meaningful). $\blacksquare$ (This makes rigorous, with an explicit vector limit, the "long-run behavior tracks the dominant eigenvalue" intuition from Day 12 Exercise 8.) | **The right coordinates.** The key move is to stop thinking of $x_0$ as a column of numbers and think of it in the eigenbasis: $x_0 = av_1 + bv_2$ gives $x_0$ the coordinates $(a,b)$ along the two eigendirections. In *these* coordinates $A^n$ is diagonal — it just multiplies each coordinate by that direction's eigenvalue raised to the $n$. <br> **Why each eigenvector behaves as claimed.** Along $v_1$: $\lambda_1=1$, and $1^n=1$, so $A^n v_1 = 1^n v_1 = v_1$ — this direction is frozen, untouched no matter how many times you apply $A$. Along $v_2$: $A^n v_2 = c^n v_2$ (Theorem 12.1), and since $\vert c\vert <1$ the scalar $c^n$ shrinks geometrically to $0$. <br> **Combine by linearity.** $A^n$ acts on the sum term-by-term: $x_n = aA^nv_1 + bA^nv_2 = av_1 + bc^nv_2$. So in eigen-coordinates the state is $(a,\,bc^n)$. <br> **Intuition — why $b$ is irrelevant.** The second coordinate is $bc^n$. Whatever finite value $b$ has, multiplying it by $c^n\to 0$ crushes it to zero; a fixed vector $v_2$ scaled by a vanishing number vanishes. Only the frozen $v_1$-component survives. Geometrically: repeated application of $A$ contracts the whole plane toward the $\lambda=1$ eigenline, so every trajectory (with $a\neq 0$) homes in on the point $av_1$ on that line. This is the rigorous version of "the dominant eigenvalue wins the long run" — here $\lambda_1=1$ dominates because $\vert c\vert <1$. <br> **What just happened:** in eigen-coordinates $A^n$ freezes the $v_1$-part and geometrically shrinks the $v_2$-part to nothing, so every start collapses onto $av_1$. |

### Exercise 15 — $A$ diagonalizable and invertible $\Rightarrow A^{-1}=PD^{-1}P^{-1}$ is diagonalizable

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Since $A$ is invertible and $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $P$ invertible, $D = P^{-1}AP$ is a product of invertible matrices, hence invertible (Theorem 9.1(b) extended to three factors). A diagonal matrix is invertible if and only if every diagonal entry is nonzero, so $D^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(1/\lambda_1, \dots, 1/\lambda_n)$ is well-defined. Now check $PD^{-1}P^{-1}$ satisfies the defining condition for an inverse of $A$: $(PDP^{-1})(PD^{-1}P^{-1}) = PD(P^{-1}P)D^{-1}P^{-1} = PD\,I\,D^{-1}P^{-1} = P(DD^{-1})P^{-1} = PIP^{-1} = I,$ $(PD^{-1}P^{-1})(PDP^{-1}) = PD^{-1}(P^{-1}P)DP^{-1} = P(D^{-1}D)P^{-1} = PIP^{-1} = I.$ Both products equal $I$, so by Definition 9.1, $PD^{-1}P^{-1}$ is an inverse of $A$; by uniqueness of the inverse (Theorem 9.1a), $A^{-1}=PD^{-1}P^{-1}$. Since $P$ is invertible and $D^{-1}$ is diagonal, this exhibits $A^{-1}$ in exactly the form Definition 11.2 requires: $A^{-1}$ is diagonalizable. $\blacksquare$ | **Step 1 — Why $D$ is invertible.** $D = P^{-1}AP$ is a product of three invertible matrices ($P^{-1}$, $A$, $P$), and a product of invertibles is invertible (Theorem 9.1(b)). For a *diagonal* matrix, invertibility means every diagonal entry — every eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ — is nonzero, which is exactly why $D^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(1/\lambda_1,\dots,1/\lambda_n)$ makes sense. (This is where "$A$ invertible" is used: it guarantees $0$ is not an eigenvalue, so no $1/\lambda_i$ blows up.) <br> **Step 2 — The one trick in the verification.** To confirm $PD^{-1}P^{-1}$ inverts $A=PDP^{-1}$, multiply and watch the inner $P^{-1}P$ telescope to $I$: $(PDP^{-1})(PD^{-1}P^{-1}) = PD(P^{-1}P)D^{-1}P^{-1} = PDD^{-1}P^{-1} = PP^{-1}=I$. The reverse product cancels the same way. Both equal $I$, so by uniqueness of the inverse (Theorem 9.1a) $A^{-1}=PD^{-1}P^{-1}$. <br> **Step 3 — Why that finishes it.** $A^{-1}=PD^{-1}P^{-1}$ has the shape $P(\text{diagonal})P^{-1}$ with the *same* $P$, which is precisely Definition 11.2's form for being diagonalizable. <br> **Intuition.** Diagonalization is a change of viewpoint: $P$ says "look at the eigenbasis," where $A$ is just the scaling list $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$. Undoing a scaling is scaling by the reciprocals — so from the eigenbasis, inverting $A$ is trivial. Because inverting reuses the *same* eigendirections, $A^{-1}$ has the same eigenvectors as $A$; only the eigenvalues flip to $1/\lambda_i$. <br> **What just happened:** in the eigenbasis, inverting $A$ just replaces each eigenvalue by its reciprocal — same eigenvectors, so $A^{-1}$ is diagonalized by the same $P$. |

### Exercise 16 — $A^3 = A \Rightarrow \det(A) \in \{-1,0,1\}$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| By Theorem 8.1 (multiplicativity of $\det$), $\det(A^3) = \det(A)\det(A)\det(A) = \det(A)^3$. Since $A^3=A$, $\det(A^3)=\det(A)$, so $\det(A)^3 = \det(A) \implies \det(A)^3 - \det(A) = 0 \implies \det(A)\big(\det(A)-1\big)\big(\det(A)+1\big) = 0$ (factoring $x^3-x = x(x-1)(x+1)$ with $x=\det(A)$). Since $\det(A)$ is a real number and this product of three real factors is $0$, at least one factor must vanish: $\det(A) = 0$, $\det(A)=1$, or $\det(A)=-1$. Hence $\det(A) \in \{-1,0,1\}$. $\blacksquare$ | **Step 1 — Turn a matrix equation into a scalar one.** The determinant is multiplicative (Theorem 8.1): $\det(XY)=\det(X)\det(Y)$. Applied twice, $\det(A^3)=\det(A)^3$. Taking $\det$ of both sides of $A^3=A$ gives $\det(A)^3=\det(A)$ — a single equation in the *number* $\det(A)$, no matrices left. <br> **Step 2 — Solve the cubic.** Let $x=\det(A)$. Then $x^3=x$, i.e. $x^3-x=0$, i.e. $x(x-1)(x+1)=0$. A product of real numbers is zero only if one factor is zero, so $x\in\{0,1,-1\}$. <br> **Intuition.** The determinant is a *homomorphism* from matrices to numbers: it turns matrix products into ordinary products. So any polynomial identity the matrix satisfies ($A^3=A$) is inherited by its determinant as the *same* polynomial identity ($x^3=x$) — and a cubic has at most three roots. This is why the possible values are so restricted even though $A$ can be enormous. <br> **What just happened:** $\det$ converts $A^3=A$ into the scalar equation $x^3=x$, whose only real solutions are $-1,0,1$. |

### Exercise 17 — Left cancellation: $A$ invertible and $AB=AC \Rightarrow B=C$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Since $A$ is invertible, $A^{-1}$ exists. Starting from $AB=AC$, multiply both sides on the left by $A^{-1}$: $A^{-1}(AB) = A^{-1}(AC) \implies (A^{-1}A)B = (A^{-1}A)C \implies IB = IC \implies B = C,$ using associativity of matrix multiplication and $A^{-1}A=I$. $\blacksquare$ | **Step 1 — Why we may multiply by $A^{-1}$.** $A$ is invertible, so $A^{-1}$ exists; applying the same matrix to both sides of a true equation keeps it true. <br> **Step 2 — Regroup and collapse.** $A^{-1}(AB)=(A^{-1}A)B$ by associativity, and $A^{-1}A=I$, so the left side becomes $IB=B$; identically the right side becomes $C$. Hence $B=C$. <br> **Intuition.** Numbers let you "divide out" a nonzero factor: $ab=ac \Rightarrow b=c$. Matrices allow the same move, but with two cautions the proof quietly respects. First, "divide" means "multiply by the inverse," which only exists when $A$ is invertible — for a non-invertible $A$, $AB=AC$ genuinely can happen with $B\neq C$. Second, matrix multiplication is not commutative, so *side matters*: because $A$ sits on the **left** of both $B$ and $C$, we must hit both sides from the **left** with $A^{-1}$ so that $A^{-1}A$ meets and cancels. <br> **What just happened:** invertibility lets you cancel $A$ from the left, exactly like dividing by a nonzero number, and the left/right bookkeeping is what makes the cancellation land. |
