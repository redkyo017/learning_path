### Exercise 4 — rank of a symmetric matrix = number of nonzero eigenvalues

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **4.** By the Spectral Theorem, $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $Q$ orthogonal (hence invertible, $Q^{-1}=Q^T$ by Day 17) and $\Lambda$ diagonal. Multiplying a matrix on the left or right by an invertible matrix does not change its rank: if $M$ is invertible, $\operatorname{rank}(MB) = \operatorname{rank}(B)$ (left-multiplying by $M$ is a bijection on $\mathbb{R}^n$, so it maps the column space of $B$ bijectively onto the column space of $MB$, preserving dimension) and likewise $\operatorname{rank}(BM)=\operatorname{rank}(B)$ (apply the previous fact to transposes: $\operatorname{rank}(BM)=\operatorname{rank}((BM)^T) = \operatorname{rank}(M^TB^T) = \operatorname{rank}(B^T) = \operatorname{rank}(B)$, using $M^T$ invertible whenever $M$ is). Applying this twice, $\operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}(Q\Lambda Q^T) = \operatorname{rank}(\Lambda Q^T) = \operatorname{rank}(\Lambda).$ For a diagonal matrix $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots, \lambda_n)$, the columns are $\lambda_ie_i$; a column is zero exactly when $\lambda_i=0$, and the nonzero columns (one per nonzero $\lambda_i$) are automatically linearly independent (distinct standard basis directions), so $\operatorname{rank}(\Lambda)$ = the number of nonzero $\lambda_i$, i.e. the number of nonzero eigenvalues of $A$ counted with multiplicity. Combining, $\operatorname{rank}(A)$ equals the number of nonzero eigenvalues of $A$ (with multiplicity). $\blacksquare$ Applying this to $A_2$ from Exercise 2, whose eigenvalues are $5, 0$: exactly one nonzero eigenvalue, so $\operatorname{rank}(A_2)=1$ — consistent with $A_2=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ having row 2 equal to $\tfrac12$ times row 1. | **Intuition.** The whole argument is: the Spectral Theorem lets you look at $A$ in a rotated coordinate system where it becomes the diagonal $\Lambda$, and rank is a coordinate-free quantity (dimension of the image), so it can't change under the rotation. In that clean frame the answer is obvious — a diagonal matrix just stretches each axis $e_i$ by $\lambda_i$, killing an axis exactly when $\lambda_i=0$. <br> **Why the rank doesn't move.** $Q$ and $Q^T$ are orthogonal, hence invertible bijections of $\mathbb{R}^n$; a bijection carries a subspace to a subspace of the same dimension, so applying it to the column space of $\Lambda$ (left factor $Q$) or reshuffling the domain (right factor $Q^T$) leaves the dimension of the image — the rank — untouched. This is the "sandwich by invertibles preserves rank" fact, and orthogonal matrices are the cleanest case of it. <br> **Why $\operatorname{rank}(\Lambda)$ = count of nonzero $\lambda_i$.** The image of $\Lambda$ is spanned by the surviving axes $\{e_i : \lambda_i \neq 0\}$, which are mutually perpendicular unit directions and therefore independent — no cancellation is possible — so the dimension is simply how many survive. <br> **What just happened:** rotating $A$ into its diagonal $\Lambda$ can't change its rank, and a diagonal matrix's rank is just how many diagonal entries are nonzero — so $\operatorname{rank}(A)$ counts the nonzero eigenvalues. |

### Exercise 5 — Trap: is every diagonalizable matrix orthogonally diagonalizable?

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **5.** **False.** Take $B=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$ from Day 11's worked example: $p_B(\lambda)=(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)$, eigenvector $(1,1)$ for $\lambda=5$ and $(1,-2)$ for $\lambda=2$, so $B$ is diagonalizable ($B=PDP^{-1}$ with $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}$, $D=\operatorname{diag}(5,2)$, verified in Day 11). But $\langle(1,1),(1,-2) \rangle = 1-2=-1\neq0$: the eigenvectors are **not orthogonal**, so $P$ cannot be replaced by an orthogonal matrix to diagonalize $B$. This is not a coincidence: if $B$ were orthogonally diagonalizable, $B=Q\Lambda Q^T$ for orthogonal $Q$, then $B^T=(Q\Lambda Q^T)^T=Q\Lambda^T Q^T=Q\Lambda Q^T=B$ would force $B$ to be symmetric — but $B^T=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix} \neq B$, so $B$ is not symmetric, and hence (by this same argument, applied in general) $B$ can *never* be orthogonally diagonalized, regardless of which $Q$ is tried. Orthogonal diagonalizability is equivalent to symmetry, not to diagonalizability alone. | **Intuition.** The trap conflates two different privileges. "Diagonalizable" only needs $n$ independent eigenvectors — they can lean at any angle. "Orthogonally diagonalizable" demands the stronger gift the Spectral Theorem hands out for free: eigenvectors that are actually *perpendicular*, so the change-of-basis matrix is a rotation/reflection ($Q^{-1}=Q^T$). $B$ has the first but not the second — its eigenvectors are independent yet skew. <br> **Why the counterexample is decisive.** Explicit is good (the two eigenvectors have dot product $-1 \neq 0$, so no orthogonal $P$ exists for *this* eigenbasis), but the slick permanent reason is the transpose test: any $A=Q\Lambda Q^T$ with orthogonal $Q$ satisfies $A^T=(Q\Lambda Q^T)^T = Q\Lambda^T Q^T = Q\Lambda Q^T = A$ because a diagonal $\Lambda$ is its own transpose. So orthogonal diagonalizability *forces* $A=A^T$. This is the exact converse of the Spectral Theorem, and together they give the clean equivalence "orthogonally diagonalizable $\iff$ symmetric." Since $B \neq B^T$, no choice of $Q$ can ever work — you don't have to keep hunting. <br> **What just happened:** diagonalizable only means independent eigenvectors, but orthogonal diagonalization requires *perpendicular* ones, and $A=Q\Lambda Q^T$ automatically makes $A$ symmetric — so the non-symmetric $B$ fails, permanently. |

### Exercise 8 — trace = sum of eigenvalues for a symmetric matrix

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** By the Spectral Theorem, $A=Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$. Using Day 11's cyclic property of trace, $\operatorname{trace}(XY)=\operatorname{trace}(YX)$, with $X=Q$ and $Y=\Lambda Q^T$: $\operatorname{trace}(A) = \operatorname{trace}(Q(\Lambda Q^T)) = \operatorname{trace}((\Lambda Q^T)Q) = \operatorname{trace}(\Lambda(Q^TQ)) = \operatorname{trace}(\Lambda I) = \operatorname{trace}(\Lambda),$ using $Q^TQ=I$ (orthogonality of $Q$) and associativity to regroup $(\Lambda Q^T)Q = \Lambda(Q^TQ)$. Finally $\operatorname{trace}(\Lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i$ directly from the definition of trace applied to a diagonal matrix. So $\operatorname{trace}(A) = \lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n$. $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition.** Trace is basis-independent: it measures something intrinsic about the map, not about the grid you wrote it in. The Spectral Theorem lets you rewrite $A$ in its eigen-frame as $\Lambda$, and in that frame the trace is literally the list of eigenvalues down the diagonal. The cyclic-trace identity is the tool that lets you slide the change-of-basis matrices out of the way to reach that frame. <br> **Why cycling is the right move.** $\operatorname{trace}(XY)=\operatorname{trace}(YX)$ lets you rotate the *last* factor to the *front*: taking $X=Q$, $Y=\Lambda Q^T$, the trailing $Q$ moves next to $Q^T$ and they annihilate as $Q^TQ=I$ (this is precisely the orthogonality gift of the Spectral Theorem — no general inverse needed). What's left is $\operatorname{trace}(\Lambda I)=\operatorname{trace}(\Lambda)$, the bare sum of eigenvalues. <br> **Sanity check.** For the worked example $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $\operatorname{trace}=4=3+1=\lambda_1+\lambda_2$. <br> **What just happened:** trace doesn't care what basis you use, so viewing $A$ in its eigen-frame $\Lambda$ (via the cyclic-trace trick that cancels $Q^TQ=I$) shows the trace is just the sum of the eigenvalues. |

### Exercise 9 — why symmetric matrices are never defective

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **9.** The Remark after Theorem 19.3 shows the induction proof never compares geometric and algebraic multiplicity at all — at each recursive step it extracts exactly one eigenvector and orthogonally deflates the problem by one dimension, and this always succeeds (Step 1 always finds a real eigenvalue, by Theorem 19.1 plus the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra). Running the recursion to completion for an $n\times n$ symmetric matrix always produces a full set of $n$ mutually orthonormal eigenvectors, so for symmetric matrices geometric multiplicity is *automatically* forced to equal algebraic multiplicity for every eigenvalue — there's no matrix for which it could fail. Day 11's $A_5=\begin{pmatrix}4&1&0\\0&4&1\\0&0&4 \end{pmatrix}$ is **not symmetric** ($A_5^T=\begin{pmatrix}4&0&0\\1&4&0\\ 0&1&4\end{pmatrix}\neq A_5$), so none of today's theorems apply to it — the Spectral Theorem's guarantee is specifically a consequence of symmetry (via Theorem 19.1's realness and the induction's repeated use of it), and $A_5$ is exactly the kind of matrix (a Jordan block) where that guarantee fails to hold precisely because the hypothesis $A=A^T$ fails. | **Intuition.** "Defective" means an eigenvalue whose eigenspace is too small — fewer independent eigenvectors than its multiplicity demands — so you can't assemble a full eigenbasis and diagonalization dies. The reason a general matrix *can* be defective is that peeling off one eigenvector might leave a smaller block that no longer hands you a clean eigenvector. The induction in Theorem 19.3 shows this can never stall for a symmetric matrix, and the deep reason is that symmetry is *self-reproducing*: the deflated block $C=V^TAV$ restricted to the orthogonal complement is again symmetric, so it again has a guaranteed real eigenvector, all the way down. <br> **Why the count comes out exactly right.** Because the recursion always succeeds $n$ times and each new eigenvector is built orthogonal to all previous ones, you get a full orthonormal set of $n$ eigenvectors — enough to force every eigenspace to its maximum possible size. Geometric multiplicity can never *exceed* algebraic multiplicity (Day 11), so being forced up to a full basis pins it *equal*. There is simply no room for a shortfall. <br> **Why $A_5$ escapes the guarantee.** The single hypothesis powering everything is $A=A^T$: it is what makes eigenvalues real (Theorem 19.1) and what makes each deflated block symmetric again. $A_5$ is a Jordan block with $A_5 \neq A_5^T$, so not one link in that chain applies — and indeed its eigenvalue $4$ has algebraic multiplicity $3$ but only a $1$-dimensional eigenspace. Symmetry isn't decoration here; it is the exact property that rules defectiveness out. <br> **What just happened:** symmetry regenerates itself at every deflation step, so the induction always finds a full orthonormal eigenbasis and no eigenspace can come up short — a luxury the non-symmetric Jordan block $A_5$ never gets. |

### Exercise 10 — Bonus: symmetric + orthogonal ⇒ eigenvalues ±1

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **10.** Since $A$ is orthogonal, $A^TA=I$, i.e. $A^{-1}=A^T$. Since $A$ is also symmetric, $A^T=A$. Combining, $A^{-1}=A$, i.e. $A\cdot A = A A^{-1} = I$, so $A^2=I$. Let $\lambda$ be any eigenvalue of $A$ with eigenvector $v\neq0$: $Av=\lambda v$. By Theorem 19.1, $\lambda$ is real (or directly: $A$ symmetric $\Rightarrow$ real eigenvalues). Apply $A$ again: $A^2v = A(\lambda v) = \lambda(Av) = \lambda^2v$. But $A^2=I$, so $A^2v=v$. Hence $\lambda^2v = v$, and since $v\neq0$, $\lambda^2=1$, giving $\lambda=\pm1$. $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition.** The two hypotheses pull the inverse in opposite directions and collide: orthogonality says $A^{-1}=A^T$, symmetry says $A^T=A$, so $A$ is its own inverse — an *involution*, $A^2=I$. Geometrically such a map is a reflection: do it twice and you're back where you started. A reflection can only scale an eigen-direction by a factor that squares to $1$, which forces $\pm1$. <br> **Why the eigenvalue is trapped.** Whatever a single application of $A$ does to an eigenvector ($v \mapsto \lambda v$), applying it twice multiplies by $\lambda^2$; but $A^2=I$ leaves $v$ fixed, so $\lambda^2 v = v$. Since $v\neq 0$ we can cancel it (an eigenvector is nonzero by definition), giving $\lambda^2=1$. Symmetry also guarantees $\lambda$ is real (Theorem 19.1), so there is no complex fourth root to worry about — only $\lambda=\pm 1$. <br> **What just happened:** being both symmetric and orthogonal makes $A$ its own inverse ($A^2=I$, a reflection), and reflecting twice can only leave an eigen-direction scaled by $\pm1$. |
