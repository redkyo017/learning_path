### Exercise 7 — invertible $A$ forces $N(A) = \{0\}$ and $N(A^T) = \{0\}$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **7.** *Direct argument.* Suppose $Ax = 0$. Since $A$ is invertible, multiply both sides on the left by $A^{-1}$: $A^{-1}(Ax) = A^{-1}0 \implies (A^{-1}A)x = 0 \implies Ix = 0 \implies x = 0$. So $N(A) = \{0\}$. For $A^T$: since $A$ is invertible, $A^{-1}$ exists with $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$. Transposing, $(AA^{-1})^T = (A^{-1})^T A^T = I^T = I$ and similarly $A^T(A^{-1})^T = I$, so $A^T$ is invertible with $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$. Applying the same argument as above with $A^T$ in place of $A$: $A^Ty = 0 \implies y = (A^T)^{-1}(A^Ty) = (A^T)^{-1}0 = 0$. So $N(A^T) = \{0\}$. *Cross-check via the Fundamental Theorem.* An invertible $n\times n$ matrix has $\operatorname{rank}(A) = n$ (its columns must span $\mathbb{R}^n$, since $A$ is onto — for every $b$, $Ax=b$ has the solution $x=A^{-1}b$ — so $C(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$ forces $r=n$). With $m=n=r$: $\dim N(A) = n-r=0$ and $\dim N(A^T)=m-r=0$, agreeing with the direct argument. | **Intuition — what the argument is really doing.** $N(A)$ (Definition 6.1) is exactly the set of inputs the map $x \mapsto Ax$ crushes to zero. Invertibility means this map is a bijection: it has an exact undo, $A^{-1}$. A map with an undo cannot merge two different inputs into one output — in particular it cannot merge any nonzero $x$ with $0$ — so the *only* thing landing on $0$ is $0$ itself. The algebra "$A^{-1}(Ax)=x$" is just that undo written out: apply $A^{-1}$ to $Ax=0$ and you recover the input, which was forced to be $0$. <br> **Why the $A^T$ half is free.** Rather than re-run everything, the solution first upgrades a fact about $A$ into a fact about $A^T$: transposing $A^{-1}A=I$ shows $A^T$ is *also* invertible, with inverse $(A^{-1})^T$. Since $N(A^T)$ is by definition the null space of the matrix $A^T$ (Definition 6.1), the identical bijection argument applies verbatim to $A^T$. <br> **Why the dimension cross-check must agree.** An invertible $n\times n$ matrix is onto, so $C(A)=\mathbb{R}^n$ and $r = \dim C(A) = n$. Feeding $m=n=r$ into Theorem 6.1 gives $\dim N(A)=n-r=0$ and $\dim N(A^T)=m-r=0$ — and a subspace of dimension $0$ is precisely $\{0\}$. The two routes are one truth seen twice: "no collapse" (bijection) and "no leftover dimension" ($r=n$). <br> **What just happened:** an invertible matrix squashes nothing to zero, so both its null spaces shrink to just the zero vector — and rank $=n$ makes the Fundamental Theorem say the same thing by counting. |

### Exercise 8 — why $\operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}(A^T)$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** Row reducing $A$ to echelon form $U$ makes both the row rank and column rank of $A$ visible at once: the number of pivots $r$ simultaneously (i) counts the nonzero (independent, spanning) rows of $U$, which is $\dim(\text{row space})$, and (ii) marks $r$ columns of the *original* $A$ that are independent and span $C(A)$, because every other column of $A$ is forced — by the very same row operations, which preserve $Ax=0 \iff Ux=0$ — to be that same combination of pivot columns as it is in $U$. Since both quantities come out of the identical set of pivots from one computation, they can't help but be equal: $\operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}(A^T)$ is not a coincidence to verify separately, it's a restatement of what the pivots already told you. | **Intuition — translate the claim first.** $\operatorname{rank}(A^T)$ is the column rank of $A^T$; but the columns of $A^T$ are the rows of $A$, so $\operatorname{rank}(A^T) = \dim(\text{row space of }A)$. The claim "$\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(A^T)$" is therefore literally "row rank $=$ column rank" — which is exactly Lemma 6.1(a)+(b): both equal the pivot count $r$. <br> **Why one row reduction settles both counts.** The point is that a *single* $U$ answers two different questions at once. (i) Rows: row operations only swap rows for reversible combinations of rows, so they never move the row space (Lemma 6.1a); the $r$ nonzero rows of $U$ are its basis, so row rank $=r$. (ii) Columns: here the bridge is that $U=EA$ with $E$ invertible, so $A$ and $U$ share a null space, $Ax=0 \iff Ux=0$. A null-space vector is nothing but a recipe for a linear dependence among columns — so $A$'s columns have *exactly the same* dependence pattern as $U$'s. In $U$ the dependence pattern is obvious to read off (non-pivot columns are combinations of earlier pivot columns), and it transfers back to $A$ unchanged, making the $r$ pivot columns of $A$ a basis for $C(A)$ (Lemma 6.1b), so column rank $=r$. <br> **Why they cannot disagree.** Both numbers are literally "count the pivots of $U$." They are two readings of one tally, not two measurements that happen to coincide — so equality needs no separate verification. <br> **What just happened:** one row reduction hands you a row basis and a column basis simultaneously, both of size $r$, so a matrix and its transpose always have the same rank. |
