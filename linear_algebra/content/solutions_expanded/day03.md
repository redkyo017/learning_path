### Exercise 8 — The identity map's matrix is $I_n$ in any basis (used on both sides)

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Let $\mathcal{B} = (v_1, \dots, v_n)$ be a basis of $V$, used as both the domain and codomain basis for $I: V \to V$. By Definition 3.2, the $j$-th column of the matrix of $I$ relative to $\mathcal{B}$ (on both sides) is the coordinate vector of $I(v_j)$ relative to $\mathcal{B}$. But $I(v_j) = v_j$ by definition of the identity map, and the coordinate vector of $v_j$ relative to the basis $\mathcal{B} = (v_1,\dots,v_n)$ is $(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)^T$ with the $1$ in position $j$ — this is because $v_j = \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{ij} v_i$ is (trivially) a linear combination of the basis vectors, and by uniqueness of basis representations, this must be *the* representation. So the $j$-th column of the matrix is the $j$-th standard basis vector of $\mathbb{R}^n$, for every $j = 1,\dots,n$. A matrix whose $j$-th column is the $j$-th standard basis vector for every $j$ is, by definition, the $n\times n$ identity matrix $I_n$. $\blacksquare$ | **The claim to hold onto.** The result is *not* special to a "nice" basis — it holds for *any* basis $\mathcal{B}$, no matter how skewed the $v_j$ look geometrically, *provided the same $\mathcal{B}$ is used on both the input and output side*. That last proviso is the whole game (contrast Exercise 10, where the sides differ and the identity gets a non-identity matrix). <br> **Why the key step works — coordinates are relative to the chosen basis.** Definition 3.2 says column $j$ records $I(v_j)$ *in $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates*, i.e. as coefficients rebuilding it from $v_1,\dots,v_n$. The identity leaves $v_j$ fixed, so we must expand $v_j$ itself in $\mathcal{B}$. But a basis vector expressed in its *own* basis is trivial: $v_j = 0\cdot v_1 + \dots + 1\cdot v_j + \dots + 0\cdot v_n$, and uniqueness of basis representations (the defining property of a basis) forbids any other set of coefficients. So the coordinate vector is $e_j$, the $j$-th standard vector of $\mathbb{R}^n$ — regardless of what $v_j$ "is" as a geometric arrow. <br> **The intuition.** Think of $[\,\cdot\,]_{\mathcal B}$ as a translator that reports every vector by its recipe in $\mathcal{B}$. Even a slanted basis vector like $(1,1)$ reports itself as "one unit of the first basis vector, zero of the rest" $=(1,0)$. The matrix therefore never sees the geometry of the $v_j$; it only hears "basis vector $j$ maps to basis vector $j$," which is exactly the pattern that spells $I_n$ column by column. <br> **What just happened:** because the identity fixes each basis vector and each basis vector reads as $e_j$ in its own coordinates, the matrix is forced to be $I_n$ — for every basis, as long as you use it on both sides. |
