### Exercise 4 — Products of orthogonal matrices are orthogonal; $Q^{-1}=Q^T$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| (a) Since $Q_1, Q_2$ are orthogonal, $Q_1^TQ_1 = I$ and $Q_2^TQ_2 = I$. Then $(Q_1Q_2)^T(Q_1Q_2) = Q_2^TQ_1^TQ_1Q_2 = Q_2^T(Q_1^TQ_1)Q_2 = Q_2^TIQ_2 = Q_2^TQ_2 = I,$ so $Q_1Q_2$ is orthogonal. <br><br> (b) $Q^TQ = I$ means, by definition of matrix inverse, that $Q^T$ is a left inverse of $Q$. For square matrices, a one-sided inverse is a two-sided inverse (a standard fact: if $BA=I$ for square $A,B$ then also $AB=I$, since $BA=I$ forces $A$ injective hence invertible, and then $B = BAA^{-1}=A^{-1}$). So $Q^TQ=I$ implies $Q$ is invertible with $Q^{-1}=Q^T$. | **The idea behind (a).** By Theorem 17.1, an orthogonal matrix is exactly a map that preserves every inner product. Composing two such maps must again preserve inner products — apply $Q_2$ (no distortion), then $Q_1$ (no distortion), and lengths/angles survive both. The algebra just makes that obvious observation precise. <br><br> **Why the algebra works.** The one non-obvious step is $(Q_1Q_2)^T = Q_2^TQ_1^T$ — transpose reverses order (Day 3's transpose rule). After that, associativity lets you regroup as $Q_2^T(Q_1^TQ_1)Q_2$; the inner factor collapses to $I$ by Definition 17.1 applied to $Q_1$, leaving $Q_2^TQ_2 = I$ by Definition 17.1 applied to $Q_2$. So $Q_1Q_2$ satisfies the defining equation $M^TM=I$. <br><br> **The idea behind (b).** For a square matrix, "has a left inverse" is already the whole story — you never have to separately check the right inverse. Intuitively, $Q^TQ=I$ says $Q$ loses no information (it is injective: if $Qx=0$ then $x=Q^TQx=0$), and an injective square map is automatically invertible, at which point its unique inverse must be the left inverse you already have, $Q^T$. This is why $Q^{-1}=Q^T$ is "free" for orthogonal matrices — you transpose instead of inverting. <br><br> **What just happened:** orthogonality is closed under multiplication (stacking rigid motions gives a rigid motion), and because orthogonal matrices are square with a left inverse, that left inverse $Q^T$ is the genuine two-sided inverse. |

### Exercise 6 — $\det(Q) = \pm 1$ for orthogonal $Q$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Since $Q$ is orthogonal, $Q^TQ=I$, so $\det(Q^TQ) = \det(I) = 1$. By multiplicativity of determinant, $\det(Q^TQ) = \det(Q^T)\det(Q)$, and since $\det(Q^T)=\det(Q)$ for any square matrix, this is $\det(Q)^2$. So $\det(Q)^2 = 1$, giving $\det(Q) = \pm1$. | **What the argument is really doing.** The determinant measures the signed volume-scaling factor of a map. An orthogonal matrix is a rigid motion — it cannot stretch or shrink anything (Theorem 17.1's corollary: it preserves all lengths). A rigid motion can only preserve volume, so its volume factor must have magnitude $1$; the sign records whether orientation is kept ($+1$, a rotation) or flipped ($-1$, a reflection). The determinant computation below confirms exactly this. <br><br> **Why each step is legal.** Starting from the defining equation $Q^TQ=I$ (Definition 17.1), take determinants of both sides — legal because equal matrices have equal determinants. The left side factors as $\det(Q^T)\det(Q)$ by multiplicativity (Day 8), and $\det(Q^T)=\det(Q)$ (also Day 8), so the left side is $\det(Q)^2$; the right side is $\det(I)=1$. Hence $\det(Q)^2=1$, and the only real numbers squaring to $1$ are $\pm1$. <br><br> **What just happened:** because a rigid motion preserves volume, its determinant can only be $+1$ (rotation, orientation kept) or $-1$ (reflection, orientation flipped) — and $Q^TQ=I$ forces exactly $\det(Q)^2=1$. |

### Exercise 7 — Orthogonal maps preserve angles, not just lengths

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| By Theorem 17.1, $\langle Qu,Qv\rangle = \langle u,v\rangle$ for all $u,v$, and by the Corollary, $\|Qu\|=\|u\|$, $\|Qv\|=\|v\|$. The angle $\theta$ between two nonzero vectors $x,y$ is defined by $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\langle x,y\rangle}{\|x\|\|y\|}$. Substituting $x=Qu,y=Qv$: $\cos(\text{angle between } Qu,Qv) = \frac{\langle Qu,Qv\rangle}{\|Qu\|\|Qv\|} = \frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|\|v\|} = \cos(\text{angle between }u,v).$ Since both angles lie in $[0,\pi]$ where cosine is injective, the angles themselves are equal. So $Q$ preserves not just individual lengths but the angle between every pair of vectors — lengths are just the special case $u=v$ (angle $0$). | **Why length-preservation alone would not be enough.** A map could conceivably keep every vector's length while still shearing the space so that two vectors that were perpendicular end up at $60°$. What rules this out is that $Q$ preserves the *inner product itself* (Theorem 17.1), which is a stronger statement than preserving lengths. The angle is built entirely out of the inner product and the two lengths — the formula $\cos\theta = \langle x,y\rangle / (\|x\|\|y\|)$ — so if $Q$ leaves all three ingredients untouched, the angle cannot budge. That is the whole conceptual content: angle is a *function of* inner product and lengths, and $Q$ fixes each input. <br><br> **Reading the key line.** Substituting $x=Qu$, $y=Qv$ into the angle formula, the numerator $\langle Qu,Qv\rangle$ is replaced by $\langle u,v\rangle$ (full Theorem 17.1) and each norm in the denominator by its un-transformed version (the corollary). Every piece matches its pre-image, so the two cosines are equal; and since angles between vectors are taken in $[0,\pi]$, where $\cos$ is one-to-one, equal cosines force equal angles. Length preservation is not a separate fact but the special case $u=v$: then the "angle" is $0$ and $\langle u,u\rangle=\|u\|^2$ is preserved, i.e. $\|Qu\|=\|u\|$. <br><br> **What just happened:** angle is completely determined by inner products and lengths, and an orthogonal $Q$ preserves all of those, so it preserves every angle — the general, any-dimension version of the 2D rotation picture from the primer. |

### Exercise 8 — Why $QQ^T \ne I$ for a rectangular $Q$ with orthonormal columns

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| False in general. Using $Q$ from Exercise 2: $QQ^T = \begin{pmatrix}\tfrac1{\sqrt2} & -\tfrac1{\sqrt6} \\ 0 & \tfrac2{\sqrt6} \\ \tfrac1{\sqrt2} & \tfrac1{\sqrt6}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac1{\sqrt2} & 0 & \tfrac1{\sqrt2} \\ -\tfrac1{\sqrt6} & \tfrac2{\sqrt6} & \tfrac1{\sqrt6}\end{pmatrix}$ has $(1,1)$ entry $\tfrac12+\tfrac16 = \tfrac23 \ne 1$, so $QQ^T \ne I_3$. The reason: $Q^TQ=I_n$ says the $n$ columns of $Q$ (living in $\mathbb{R}^m$) are orthonormal, which pins down an $n\times n$ product; but $QQ^T$ is $m\times m$, and when $m>n$ the columns of $Q$ span only an $n$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$, not all of it, so $QQ^T$ is actually the (rank-$n$) orthogonal projection onto that subspace, not the identity. Only when $m=n$ (so $Q$ is square) does $Q^TQ=I$ force $QQ^T=I$ too (that's Exercise 4(b): $Q^{-1}=Q^T$ on both sides). | **The core distinction: $Q^TQ$ and $QQ^T$ ask different questions.** $Q^TQ$ is the $n\times n$ Gram matrix of the *columns* — its $(i,j)$ entry is $\langle q_i,q_j\rangle$, so $Q^TQ=I_n$ is just the statement "the $n$ columns are orthonormal," which is true here. $QQ^T$ is $m\times m$ and is a different object: from Day 16, for a matrix with orthonormal columns, $QQ^T$ is precisely the orthogonal projection onto $\operatorname{col}(Q)$. When $m>n$ that column space is a proper $n$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$, so the projection has rank $n<m$ and cannot be $I_m$ (the identity has rank $m$). A projection only equals the identity when it projects onto the *whole* space — i.e. when the columns already span all of $\mathbb{R}^m$, which needs $m=n$. <br><br> **Reading the counterexample.** For the $3\times 2$ matrix from Exercise 2, the $(1,1)$ entry of $QQ^T$ is the squared length of $Q$'s *first row*, $\left(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right)^2 + \left(-\tfrac1{\sqrt6}\right)^2 = \tfrac12+\tfrac16=\tfrac23\ne1$. The rows of a tall orthonormal-column matrix are *not* unit vectors — only the columns are — which is exactly why $QQ^T\ne I_3$. In fact $QQ^T$ here is rank $2$, projecting $\mathbb{R}^3$ onto the plane spanned by the two columns. <br><br> **Why squareness fixes it.** If $m=n$, orthonormal columns span all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, the projection onto that full space is $I_n$, and separately Exercise 4(b) gives $Q^{-1}=Q^T$, so $QQ^T=QQ^{-1}=I$ automatically. The two-sided identity is a privilege of square orthogonal matrices. <br><br> **What just happened:** orthonormal *columns* only guarantee $Q^TQ=I_n$; the other product $QQ^T$ is the projection onto the columns' span, which fills all of $\mathbb{R}^m$ (and equals $I_m$) only when $Q$ is square. |
