### Exercise 2 — A symmetric matrix with all eigenvalues equal to $c$ must be $cI$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **2.** By the spectral theorem $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $\Lambda = cI$ (all eigenvalues $c$), so $A = Q(cI)Q^T = c(QQ^T) = cI$ since $Q$ is orthogonal. | **Intuition.** A general matrix with a repeated eigenvalue need *not* be a multiple of $I$ (think of a shear like $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, both eigenvalues $1$). What forces the conclusion here is *symmetry*: the spectral theorem (Day 19) hands a symmetric matrix a full orthonormal eigenbasis, so when every eigenvalue is the same $c$, the matrix scales *every* direction by $c$ — and a map that scales every direction equally is exactly $cI$. <br> **Step 1 — invoke the spectral theorem.** $A$ symmetric $\Rightarrow A=Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda$ the diagonal eigenvalue matrix (Day 19). All eigenvalues equal $c$, so $\Lambda=cI$. <br> **Step 2 — pull the scalar out.** $A=Q(cI)Q^T=c\,QIQ^T=c\,QQ^T$; a scalar commutes with everything, and $cI$ acts as multiplication by $c$. <br> **Step 3 — use orthogonality.** $Q$ orthogonal means $QQ^T=I$ (Day 17), so $A=cI$. <br> **What just happened:** repeated eigenvalues alone don't pin down a matrix, but symmetry supplies an orthonormal eigenbasis, and a symmetric map that stretches every one of those directions by the same $c$ can only be $cI$. |

### Exercise 3 — If $A$ is symmetric with $A^2=A$, every eigenvalue is $0$ or $1$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **3.** True. If $Av=\lambda v$ ($v\ne0$) and $A^2=A$, then $\lambda^2 v = A^2v = Av = \lambda v$, so $\lambda^2=\lambda$, i.e. $\lambda(\lambda-1)=0$, so $\lambda \in \{0,1\}$. | **Intuition.** A matrix with $A^2=A$ is a *projection*: applying it twice does nothing new, because after one application you already land in the target subspace. Geometrically a projection either keeps a direction (eigenvalue $1$, the directions it projects onto) or annihilates it (eigenvalue $0$, the directions it flattens). The algebra below is just that fact read off the eigenvalue equation. <br> **Step 1 — feed an eigenvector through $A$ twice.** For $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, applying $A$ again gives $A^2v=A(\lambda v)=\lambda(Av)=\lambda^2 v$. <br> **Step 2 — use the hypothesis.** $A^2=A$ forces $A^2v=Av=\lambda v$. Equating the two expressions for $A^2v$: $\lambda^2 v=\lambda v$, and since $v\ne0$ we may cancel it to get $\lambda^2=\lambda$. <br> **Step 3 — solve.** $\lambda^2-\lambda=\lambda(\lambda-1)=0$ gives $\lambda\in\{0,1\}$. <br> **What just happened:** "doing it twice equals doing it once" ($A^2=A$) means every direction is either fixed or killed — the only self-consistent stretch factors are $1$ and $0$. |

### Exercise 5 — Positive definite $\Rightarrow$ every diagonal entry is positive

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **5.** $x^TAx > 0$ for all $x\ne0$. Take $x=e_i$ (the $i$-th standard basis vector, which is nonzero): $e_i^TAe_i = A_{ii} > 0$. | **Intuition.** Positive definiteness ("$x^TAx>0$ for *all* nonzero $x$") is a statement about every direction at once, which sounds hard to use. The trick is that a universal statement lets you *choose the most convenient witness*: plug in the standard basis vectors, and the quadratic form collapses to a single matrix entry. So a global positivity condition immediately pins down each diagonal entry. <br> **Step 1 — instantiate the definition.** By definition (Day 20), $x^TAx>0$ for every $x\ne0$. Since $e_i$ (a $1$ in slot $i$, zeros elsewhere) is nonzero, the inequality applies to it: $e_i^TAe_i>0$. <br> **Step 2 — why $e_i^TAe_i=A_{ii}$.** $Ae_i$ is the $i$-th column of $A$; dotting with $e_i$ picks out the $i$-th entry of that column, namely $A_{ii}$. (Equivalently, $e_i^TAe_i=\sum_{k,l}(e_i)_k A_{kl}(e_i)_l$ and only the $k=l=i$ term survives.) <br> **Step 3 — conclude.** Combining, $A_{ii}=e_i^TAe_i>0$ for every $i$. <br> **What just happened:** testing the "positive in every direction" condition along the coordinate axes reads the diagonal entries straight off, so they must all be positive — the necessary condition the day's theory warns is *not* sufficient. |

### Exercise 8 — The singular values of an orthogonal matrix are all $1$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** $Q^TQ=I$ means $A^TA=I$ for $A=Q$, so every eigenvalue of $A^TA$ is $1$, hence every singular value $\sigma_i=\sqrt1=1$. | **Intuition.** Singular values measure how much a matrix stretches the unit sphere along its principal axes (Day 21). An orthogonal matrix is a rigid motion — a rotation/reflection — so it moves the sphere around without stretching it at all. Every axis keeps length $1$, so every singular value is $1$. The algebra below confirms this via the definition $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(A^TA)}$. <br> **Step 1 — recall the definition.** Singular values of $A$ are $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ where $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of $A^TA$ (Day 21). <br> **Step 2 — compute $A^TA$ for $A=Q$.** Orthogonality means $Q^TQ=I$ (Day 17), so $A^TA=I$. <br> **Step 3 — eigenvalues of $I$.** Every vector satisfies $Iv=1\cdot v$, so every eigenvalue of $A^TA=I$ equals $1$. <br> **Step 4 — take square roots.** $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}=\sqrt1=1$ for all $i$. <br> **What just happened:** an orthogonal matrix is a pure rotation/reflection that stretches nothing, so $A^TA$ collapses to $I$ and all singular values come out to $1$ — the algebraic shadow of "rigid motions preserve length." |

### Exercise 11 — Is the rank-$k$ truncation the *unique* best rank-$k$ approximation?

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **11.** False in general — if there's a tie among singular values at the truncation boundary (e.g. $\sigma_k=\sigma_{k+1}$), multiple rank-$k$ matrices achieve the same minimal error, since the choice of which singular vectors to include among the tied ones is not unique. | **Intuition.** Eckart–Young (Day 22) guarantees the truncation $A_k$ achieves the *minimum* error — but "minimum" and "unique minimizer" are different claims. The subtlety is where the cut falls. If the two singular values straddling the boundary are different, keeping the top $k$ is the only optimal choice. But if $\sigma_k=\sigma_{k+1}$, the $k$-th and $(k+1)$-th directions are *equally important*, so which one you keep is a coin flip — and each choice hits the same minimal error. <br> **Why a tie breaks uniqueness.** With $\sigma_k=\sigma_{k+1}=s$, the error of any rank-$k$ truncation that includes exactly one of these two tied directions is $\|A-A_k\|_F=\sqrt{\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}$ either way — dropping "the $k$-th" costs $s^2$ and dropping "the $(k+1)$-th" also costs $s^2$. More sharply, the eigenspace for the repeated singular value is $2$-dimensional, and *any* unit vector in it is a legitimate singular vector, so there is a continuum of equally-optimal $A_k$. <br> **When it IS unique.** If $\sigma_k>\sigma_{k+1}$ strictly (a genuine gap at the boundary), the top-$k$ singular directions are forced and $A_k$ is the unique minimizer. <br> **What just happened:** the truncation is *always* a best rank-$k$ approximation, but it is the *only* one precisely when there's no tie at the cutoff; a repeated singular value on the boundary makes the optimal choice ambiguous without changing the minimal error. |

### Exercise 13 — Sum of covariance eigenvalues equals total variance

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **13.** $\operatorname{trace}(C) = \sum_i \lambda_i$ (Day 26 will prove this in general; you may cite it here). But also, by definition, $C_{jj} = \operatorname{Var}(X_j)$ (the diagonal entries of a covariance matrix are the individual feature variances), so $\operatorname{trace}(C) = \sum_j C_{jj} = \sum_j \operatorname{Var}(X_j)$. Combining, $\sum_i\lambda_i = \sum_j\operatorname{Var}(X_j)$ — PCA redistributes the same total variance across orthogonal directions. |  **Intuition.** The trace is a *bridge* between two ways of describing the same matrix. Read down the diagonal, it sums the per-feature variances (how spread out each original coordinate is). Read through the eigenvalues, it sums the variances along the principal directions. Because both expressions equal the one number $\operatorname{trace}(C)$, "total variance" is an invariant — PCA just repackages the same budget of variance into new, orthogonal axes without creating or destroying any. <br> **Step 1 — trace via eigenvalues.** For any square matrix, $\operatorname{trace}(C)=\sum_i\lambda_i$ (cited from Day 26). Since $C$ is symmetric, this is especially clean: $C=Q\Lambda Q^T$ and trace is basis-independent, so the trace equals $\sum_i\lambda_i$. <br> **Step 2 — trace via the diagonal.** By definition the diagonal of a covariance matrix holds the individual feature variances, $C_{jj}=\operatorname{Var}(X_j)$, so $\operatorname{trace}(C)=\sum_j C_{jj}=\sum_j\operatorname{Var}(X_j)$. <br> **Step 3 — equate.** Both compute the same trace, so $\sum_i\lambda_i=\sum_j\operatorname{Var}(X_j)$. <br> **What just happened:** the trace is the total variance seen two ways — along the original features and along the principal axes — so PCA conserves total variance while merely redistributing it across orthogonal directions. |

### Exercise 14 — Why data must be centered before PCA

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **14.** The variance derivation (Day 23, Theorem 23.1) relies on $Xw$ being mean-zero so that $\operatorname{Var}(Xw) = \frac1{n-1}\|Xw\|^2$. If $X$ isn't centered, $\frac1{n-1}\|Xw\|^2$ conflates variance with the squared mean (it computes $E[(Xw)^2]$, not $\operatorname{Var}(Xw)=E[(Xw)^2]-E[Xw]^2$), so the eigenvectors of $X^TX$ would no longer correspond to directions of maximum *variance* — they'd be contaminated by the offset of the data cloud from the origin. | **Intuition.** PCA measures *spread*, and spread should be about how points scatter around their own center, not about how far that center sits from the origin. Variance is a squared distance from the *mean*; $\frac1{n-1}\|Xw\|^2$ is a squared distance from the *origin*. Those two agree only when the mean is at the origin — i.e. after centering. Skip centering and PCA chases the direction pointing toward the far-off data cloud instead of the direction along which the cloud is most elongated. <br> **Step 1 — what the derivation assumes.** Theorem 23.1 (Day 23) equates the variance of the projection with a squared length, $\operatorname{Var}(Xw)=\frac1{n-1}\|Xw\|^2$. That identity is valid *only when $Xw$ is mean-zero*, which requires the columns of $X$ to be centered. <br> **Step 2 — the error if you skip it.** In general $\operatorname{Var}(Xw)=E[(Xw)^2]-E[Xw]^2$. Using $\frac1{n-1}\|Xw\|^2$ computes only $E[(Xw)^2]$, silently adding back the $E[Xw]^2$ term you meant to subtract — variance is conflated with the squared mean. <br> **Step 3 — the consequence.** Maximizing that inflated quantity, the top eigenvector of $X^TX$ tilts toward wherever the cloud's mean sits relative to the origin, not toward the axis of greatest actual scatter. The principal directions get contaminated by the offset. <br> **What just happened:** centering makes "distance from origin" equal "distance from mean," so the eigenvectors of $X^TX$ track genuine variance; without it, PCA measures where the data *is* rather than how it *spreads*. |
