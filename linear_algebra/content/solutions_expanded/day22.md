### Exercise 2 — Why $\|A\|_F^2 = \|A_1\|_F^2 + \|A-A_1\|_F^2$ (a Pythagorean split)

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **2.** From the Worked Example: $\|A\|_F^2=4$, $\|A_1\|_F^2=3$, $\|A-A_1\|_F^2=1$, and indeed $3+1=4$. This holds in general because $A = A_k + (A-A_k)$, and Lemma 22.1 shows $\|A\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2$ (all $r$ terms), $\|A_k\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^k\sigma_i^2$ (the first $k$ terms), and $\|A-A_k\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=k+1}^r\sigma_i^2$ (the remaining terms) — the total sum of squares simply splits into a "kept" part and a "discarded" part, so $\|A_k\|_F^2 + \|A-A_k\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^k\sigma_i^2 + \sum_{i=k+1}^r\sigma_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r\sigma_i^2 = \|A\|_F^2$ exactly, for every valid $k$. | **The picture.** Lemma 22.1 says: for a matrix built from *orthonormal* rank-1 pieces $\sigma_i u_i v_i^T$, the Frobenius norm squared is just the sum of the squared weights $\sigma_i^2$ — nothing else, no cross terms. So $\|\cdot\|_F^2$ behaves exactly like a squared length in a coordinate system whose "axes" are the pieces $u_i v_i^T$. <br> **Why the sum splits.** $A_k$ uses the pieces $i=1,\dots,k$; the remainder $A-A_k$ uses the *disjoint* pieces $i=k+1,\dots,r$. Because these two index sets never overlap, splitting the sum $\sum_{i=1}^r\sigma_i^2$ into $\sum_{i=1}^k$ plus $\sum_{i=k+1}^r$ loses and double-counts nothing: $\|A_k\|_F^2 + \|A-A_k\|_F^2 = \|A\|_F^2$. <br> **This is literally Pythagoras.** In the Frobenius inner product $\langle X,Y\rangle=\operatorname{trace}(X^TY)$, the pieces from disjoint index sets are orthogonal, so $A_k \perp (A-A_k)$. The identity $\|A_k\|_F^2+\|A-A_k\|_F^2=\|A\|_F^2$ is the theorem of Pythagoras applied to the right triangle with legs $A_k$ and $A-A_k$ and hypotenuse $A$. <br> **What just happened:** the total "energy" $\sum\sigma_i^2$ of $A$ divides cleanly into what the truncation keeps and what it discards, because the kept and dropped pieces sit on perpendicular axes. |

### Exercise 3 — $\|X\|_F^2 = \sum_{i,j}X_{ij}^2 = \operatorname{trace}(X^TX)$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **3.** By definition, the Frobenius norm is $\|X\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} X_{ij}^2}$, so $\|X\|_F^2 = \sum_{i,j}X_{ij}^2$ directly from the definition — nothing to prove there beyond squaring. For the trace identity: $(X^TX)_{jj} = \sum_i (X^T)_{ji}X_{ij} = \sum_i X_{ij}X_{ij} = \sum_i X_{ij}^2$ (using $(X^T)_{ji}=X_{ij}$ and the definition of matrix multiplication, entry $(j,j)$ of $X^TX$ is row $j$ of $X^T$ dotted with column $j$ of $X$). Summing over the diagonal, $\operatorname{trace}(X^TX) = \sum_j (X^TX)_{jj} = \sum_j\sum_i X_{ij}^2 = \sum_{i,j}X_{ij}^2.$ Combining both computations, $\|X\|_F^2 = \sum_{i,j}X_{ij}^2 = \operatorname{trace}(X^TX)$. | **What the diagonal of $X^TX$ really is.** Entry $(j,j)$ of $X^TX$ is (column $j$ of $X$) dotted with itself, i.e. $\sum_i X_{ij}^2$ — the *squared length of column $j$*. So the diagonal of $X^TX$ is a list of "energy per column." <br> **Why the trace recovers the norm.** Summing the diagonal (that's what $\operatorname{trace}$ does) adds up the energy of every column, which is the same as adding the square of every entry, just grouped by column instead of scanned freely: $\sum_j(\text{energy of column }j)=\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}^2$. <br> **Intuition for why we bother.** The raw definition $\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}^2$ is entrywise and clumsy to manipulate; rewriting it as $\operatorname{trace}(X^TX)$ turns "size of $X$" into an algebraic object you can expand, factor, and push orthonormality through — which is exactly the move Lemma 22.1 relies on. The off-diagonal entries of $X^TX$ are thrown away by the trace, so only the diagonal (the column energies) survives. <br> **What just happened:** "sum of all squared entries" and "trace of $X^TX$" are the same number viewed two ways — scan-all-entries versus add-up-the-column-energies on the diagonal. |

### Exercise 4 — All cross terms $i\ne j$ vanish in $X^TX$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **4.** $X^TX = \left(\sum_i c_iz_iw_i^T\right)\left(\sum_j c_jw_jz_j^T\right) = \sum_{i,j} c_ic_j\, z_i(w_i^Tw_j)z_j^T$ by distributing the product over both sums (matrix multiplication is bilinear) and regrouping the scalar $w_i^Tw_j$ out of the product $z_iw_i^Tw_jz_j^T = z_i(w_i^Tw_j)z_j^T$ (since $w_i^Tw_j$ is a scalar, it commutes past the vectors). Since $w_i^Tw_j = \delta_{ij}$ by orthonormality, the term $c_ic_j\,\delta_{ij}\,z_iz_j^T$ is $c_i^2z_iz_i^T$ when $i=j$ and $0$ when $i\ne j$. Summing over all $i,j$, only the $i=j$ terms survive: $X^TX = \sum_i c_i^2 z_iz_i^T$. | **Where the middle factor comes from.** In $z_i w_i^T w_j z_j^T$, the inner pair $w_i^T w_j$ is a $1\times1$ block — a plain number. A scalar can slide freely through a matrix product, so the whole term collapses to (that number) $\times\, z_i z_j^T$. This is the only algebra happening; the outer product $z_i z_j^T$ is untouched. <br> **The gate.** Orthonormality makes $w_i^T w_j = \delta_{ij}$: the scalar is $1$ exactly on the diagonal $i=j$ and $0$ everywhere else. Think of $\delta_{ij}$ as a gate that lets a term through only when its two indices match. Of the $p^2$ terms in the double sum, the $p$ diagonal ones ($i=j$) pass as $c_i^2 z_i z_i^T$; all $p^2-p$ off-diagonal ones are multiplied by $0$ and disappear. <br> **Why it matters.** This is the exact mechanism behind Lemma 22.1's clean answer: the $z_i$'s here are *not* even assumed orthonormal, yet the cross terms still die — the collapse is powered entirely by the $w_i$'s being orthonormal. (Lemma 22.1 then adds orthonormal $z_i$'s and a trace to turn each surviving $z_i z_i^T$ into $1$.) <br> **What just happened:** the double sum looks like it has $p^2$ contributions, but orthonormality of the $w_i$'s switches off every mismatched pair, leaving only the $p$ diagonal terms $\sum_i c_i^2 z_i z_i^T$. |

### Exercise 5 — $\operatorname{rank}(A_k)=k$ exactly when $\sigma_k>0$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **5.** For any $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $A_kx = \sum_{i=1}^k\sigma_i(v_i^Tx)u_i$, which is a linear combination of $u_1,\dots,u_k$ alone; so the column space (range) of $A_k$ is contained in $\operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_k)$, giving $\operatorname{rank}(A_k) \le k$. Conversely, for each $j=1,\dots,k$, $A_kv_j = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i(v_i^Tv_j)u_i = \sigma_ju_j$ (using orthonormality $v_i^Tv_j=\delta_{ij}$). Since $\sigma_j>0$ (given, as $j\le k$ and $\sigma_k>0$ with $\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_k$), this shows $u_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_j}A_kv_j$ is itself in the column space of $A_k$, for every $j=1,\dots,k$. So $\operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_k) \subseteq$ column space of $A_k$ too. Combining both inclusions, the column space of $A_k$ equals $\operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_k)$ exactly, which has dimension $k$ (the $u_i$ are orthonormal, hence linearly independent). So $\operatorname{rank}(A_k)=k$ exactly. | **The strategy: trap the column space between two inclusions.** Rank $=$ dimension of the column space, so we pin that space from both sides. Showing "$\subseteq\operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_k)$" gives $\le k$; showing "$\supseteq$" gives $\ge k$; together the column space *is* $\operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_k)$, dimension exactly $k$. <br> **Upper bound (why $\le k$).** Every output $A_kx=\sum_{i=1}^k\sigma_i(v_i^Tx)u_i$ is some combination of just $u_1,\dots,u_k$ — no matter what $x$ is, only those $k$ directions ever appear. So $A_k$ can never reach outside their span. <br> **Lower bound (why $\ge k$) and where $\sigma_k>0$ is essential.** Probing with the input $v_j$ isolates a single output: $A_k v_j=\sigma_j u_j$, because orthonormality kills every $v_i^T v_j$ except $i=j$. Dividing by $\sigma_j$ shows $u_j$ itself is a genuine output of $A_k$ — *provided $\sigma_j\ne0$*. This is the whole role of the hypothesis: if some $\sigma_j$ were $0$, that direction $u_j$ would be crushed to $\mathbf{0}$ and would not sit in the range, and the rank could drop below $k$. Because $\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_k$ and $\sigma_k>0$, all $k$ of them are strictly positive, so all $k$ directions $u_1,\dots,u_k$ are truly hit. <br> **What just happened:** $A_k$'s outputs live in the $k$-dimensional span of $u_1,\dots,u_k$ and — since no kept singular value is zero — actually fill it, so the rank is exactly $k$, not merely at most $k$. |

### Exercise 7 — A small singular value is not a small entry

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **7.** False. Entrywise thresholding operates on individual coordinates of $A$ in the standard basis and has no relationship to $A$'s singular directions — it neither reliably reduces rank nor, when it does change rank, does so in the Frobenius-optimal way that Eckart–Young guarantees for SVD truncation. Concrete example: let $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0.01\\0.01&1 \end{pmatrix}$. The entries $0.01$ are numerically small, so entrywise thresholding (zeroing anything below, say, $0.05$) produces $I_2$ — which still has rank $2$ (full rank!), not a lower-rank matrix at all, even though $0.01$ "looks small." The notion of "small" that matters for approximation is smallness of a *singular value* (a direction in which $A$ barely stretches space), not smallness of an individual matrix *entry* — these are generally unrelated, and only the SVD-based notion has an optimality guarantee behind it (Theorem 22.2). | **The core confusion the problem targets.** "Small entry" and "small singular value" measure different things. An entry $A_{ij}$ is a coordinate in the *standard basis* — it changes if you merely rotate how you look at the matrix. A singular value $\sigma_i$ is *intrinsic*: it is how much $A$ stretches space along a genuine axis, independent of any coordinate choice. Eckart–Young (Theorem 22.2) is a statement about the intrinsic quantities only. <br> **Why the example is decisive.** For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.01\\0.01&1\end{pmatrix}$, zeroing the small entries gives $I_2$, still full rank $2$ — entry-smallness did not even lower the rank, so it cannot be "a rank-$k$ approximation" of anything. Meanwhile the actual singular values of $A$ (its eigenvalues, since it is symmetric positive definite) are $1.01$ and $0.99$ — *both* close to $1$. So there is genuinely no good rank-1 approximation here: the best one has error $\sigma_2=0.99$, nearly the size of $A$ itself. The SVD correctly reports "this matrix is essentially two-dimensional," whereas the tiny entries falsely whisper "almost rank-1." <br> **The takeaway.** What you may safely discard is a direction along which $A$ barely acts (a small $\sigma_i$), not a numerically small bookkeeping coordinate. Only the first has an optimality theorem behind it. <br> **What just happened:** deleting small entries and truncating small singular values are unrelated operations — the example shows the former can leave the rank untouched, while only the latter is provably the best low-rank fit. |
