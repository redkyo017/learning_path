### Exercise 7 — injective + equal dimension ⇒ surjective (via rank-nullity)

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **7.** Suppose $T: V\to W$ is injective and $\dim V = \dim W =: n$ (both finite). By Lemma 4.1, injectivity gives $\ker T = \{0\}$, so $\dim(\ker T) = 0$. By the rank-nullity theorem (Theorem 4.1), $\dim(\ker T) + \dim(\operatorname{im} T) = \dim V \implies 0 + \dim(\operatorname{im} T) = n \implies \dim(\operatorname{im} T) = n.$ Now $\operatorname{im} T$ is a subspace of $W$ with $\dim(\operatorname{im} T) = n = \dim W$. A subspace of a finite-dimensional space with dimension equal to the whole space must equal the whole space: extend a basis of $\operatorname{im} T$ ($n$ vectors) to a basis of $W$; but $\dim W = n$ already, so the extension adds zero vectors, meaning the original basis of $\operatorname{im} T$ was already a basis of $W$, i.e. $\operatorname{im} T = W$. Hence $T$ is surjective. $\blacksquare$ | **The idea.** Rank-nullity ($\dim(\ker T)+\dim(\operatorname{im} T)=\dim V$) is a conservation law: the $n$ input dimensions split into "collapsed to zero" (nullity) and "survives the map" (rank). <br> **Why injectivity forces full rank.** Injective means $\ker T=\{0\}$ (Lemma 4.1), so *zero* dimensions are spent on the kernel — every one of the $n$ input directions survives, giving $\dim(\operatorname{im} T)=n$. <br> **Why full rank forces surjectivity.** The image is an $n$-dimensional subspace sitting inside a codomain that *also* has dimension exactly $n$. A proper subspace would have strictly smaller dimension, so an $n$-dimensional subspace of an $n$-dimensional space has nowhere left to hide — its basis is already a maximal independent set in $W$, hence a basis of $W$, so $\operatorname{im} T=W$. <br> **Analogy.** Pour $n$ liters into an $n$-liter tank and lose none along the way; the tank must end up exactly full. <br> **What just happened:** an injective map wastes no dimension, so between spaces of equal dimension its image is forced to fill the entire target. |

### Exercise 8 — no injective linear map $\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** False. If $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ were injective, Lemma 4.1 gives $\dim(\ker T) = 0$, so rank-nullity forces $\dim(\operatorname{im} T) = \dim\mathbb{R}^3 - 0 = 3$. But $\operatorname{im} T$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$, which has dimension only $2$, and no subspace can have larger dimension than its ambient space — contradiction. So no such injective map exists. (Intuitively: you cannot inject a "bigger" space into a "smaller" one linearly, since $3$ dimensions of input can't avoid collapsing onto only $2$ dimensions of output — some nonzero vector must land in the kernel.) | **The idea.** Rank-nullity budgets the $3$ input dimensions between kernel and image. The image lives inside $\mathbb{R}^2$, so it can absorb *at most* $2$ of them: $\dim(\operatorname{im} T)\le 2$. <br> **Where injectivity breaks.** That cap forces the leftover onto the kernel: $\dim(\ker T)=3-\dim(\operatorname{im} T)\ge 3-2=1$. A kernel of dimension $\ge 1$ contains a nonzero vector, so by Lemma 4.1 $T$ cannot be injective — the argument in the original runs the same accounting and hits the contradiction $\dim(\operatorname{im} T)=3>2$. <br> **Geometry.** You are flattening 3-D space onto a 2-D screen; a whole line's worth of directions must get squashed to a point. <br> **What just happened:** three input dimensions can't all survive into a two-dimensional output, so at least one direction collapses to zero and injectivity is impossible. |

### Exercise 9 — no surjective linear map $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **9.** False. By rank-nullity, for *any* linear $T:\mathbb{R}^2\to \mathbb{R}^3$, $\dim(\operatorname{im} T) = \dim\mathbb{R}^2 - \dim(\ker T) = 2 - \dim(\ker T) \le 2 < 3 = \dim\mathbb{R}^3$. So $\operatorname{im} T$ can never be all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ — it is at most a 2-dimensional subspace (e.g. the naive attempt $T(x_1,x_2) = (x_1,x_2,0)$ has image $\operatorname{span}\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0)\}$, missing every point with nonzero third coordinate). No surjective linear map $\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3$ exists — the same "can't grow dimension" obstruction as Exercise 8, mirrored on the image side. This is the trap the exercise is testing: the naive instinct is "sure, just map onto a subspace and call it surjective," but surjective means hitting *all* of $\mathbb{R}^3$, and rank-nullity caps the image dimension at $2$. | **The idea (mirror of Ex. 8).** Now the *domain* is the bottleneck. Rank-nullity gives $\dim(\operatorname{im} T)=2-\dim(\ker T)$, and since nullity is never negative, the rank is capped by the input dimension: $\dim(\operatorname{im} T)\le 2$ — no matter how cleverly $T$ is chosen. <br> **Why that kills surjectivity.** Surjective onto $\mathbb{R}^3$ needs $\dim(\operatorname{im} T)=3$, but $2<3$, so the image is at most a plane. A plane genuinely lives in 3-space yet is not all of it (it misses everything off the plane, e.g. any point with nonzero third coordinate in the naive example). <br> **The trap.** "Map onto a subspace" is not surjective — surjective means reaching *every* target vector. <br> **What just happened:** two input dimensions can produce at most a two-dimensional image, which can never fill three-dimensional space. |

### Exercise 10 — the inverse of a composition reverses order: $(S\circ T)^{-1}=T^{-1}\circ S^{-1}$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **10.** Since $T$ is invertible, $T^{-1}: V \to U$ exists with $T^{-1}\circ T = \operatorname{id}_U$, $T\circ T^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_V$; similarly $S^{-1}: W\to V$ satisfies $S^{-1}\circ S=\operatorname{id}_V$, $S\circ S^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_W$. Both $T^{-1}$ and $S^{-1}$ are linear (by definition of invertible linear transformation), and a composition of linear maps is linear, so $T^{-1}\circ S^{-1}: W \to U$ is linear. Check it's a two-sided inverse of $S\circ T: U \to W$: $(T^{-1}\circ S^{-1})\circ(S\circ T) = T^{-1}\circ(S^{-1}\circ S)\circ T = T^{-1}\circ \operatorname{id}_V\circ T = T^{-1}\circ T = \operatorname{id}_U,$ $(S\circ T)\circ(T^{-1}\circ S^{-1}) = S\circ(T\circ T^{-1})\circ S^{-1} = S\circ \operatorname{id}_V\circ S^{-1} = S\circ S^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_W.$ So $S\circ T$ is invertible with $(S\circ T)^{-1} = T^{-1}\circ S^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$ | **Why the order flips.** $S\circ T$ means "do $T$, then $S$". To undo it you must peel off the *last* thing done first: undo $S$ (apply $S^{-1}$), then undo $T$ (apply $T^{-1}$) — so the inverse is $T^{-1}\circ S^{-1}$, not $S^{-1}\circ T^{-1}$. The everyday version: to reverse "socks then shoes" you take off shoes then socks. <br> **Why the cancellation works.** Composition is associative, so you can regroup freely (this is the only algebraic fact used). Regroup to put the inner pair adjacent: $(T^{-1}\circ S^{-1})\circ(S\circ T)=T^{-1}\circ(S^{-1}\circ S)\circ T$. The adjacent pair $S^{-1}\circ S$ collapses to $\operatorname{id}_V$, which passes transparently through, leaving $T^{-1}\circ T=\operatorname{id}_U$. The other order cancels symmetrically from the middle out. <br> **Why linearity is mentioned.** "Invertible" here means invertible *as a linear map*, so the candidate inverse must itself be linear — guaranteed because $T^{-1},S^{-1}$ are linear and composites of linear maps are linear. <br> **What just happened:** to reverse a two-step process you reverse each step in the opposite order, and associativity lets the matching inverse pairs cancel from the inside out. |
