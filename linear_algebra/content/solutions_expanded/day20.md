### Exercise 7 — Positive definite $\Rightarrow$ invertible and $A^{-1}$ positive definite

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| *Invertibility.* Since $A$ is positive definite, Theorem 20.1 says every eigenvalue of $A$ is (strictly) positive, so in particular $0$ is not an eigenvalue of $A$. By the eigenvalue/null-space correspondence (Day 10/11: $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ iff $Av=0$ for some $v \neq 0$, i.e. iff $A$ has nontrivial null space), $A$ having no eigenvalue equal to $0$ means $N(A) = \{0\}$, i.e. $A$ is invertible.<br><br>*Eigenvalues of $A^{-1}$.* Let $\lambda$ be any eigenvalue of $A$ (so $\lambda > 0$, hence $\lambda \neq 0$) with eigenvector $v \neq 0$: $Av = \lambda v$. Multiply both sides on the left by $A^{-1}$: $v = A^{-1}(\lambda v) = \lambda A^{-1}v$. Since $\lambda \neq 0$, divide both sides by $\lambda$: $A^{-1}v = \tfrac{1}{\lambda}v$. So $v$ is also an eigenvector of $A^{-1}$, with eigenvalue $1/\lambda$. Running this over every eigenvalue $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ of $A$ (all positive) shows every eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ is of the form $1/\lambda_i$ for some positive $\lambda_i$, hence itself positive.<br><br>*Conclusion.* $A^{-1}$ is symmetric: $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1} = A^{-1}$ (using $A^T=A$). Since $A^{-1}$ is symmetric with every eigenvalue positive, Theorem 20.1 (applied to $A^{-1}$ in place of $A$) says $A^{-1}$ is positive definite. $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition — definiteness is a statement about the eigenvalue *signs*, and inverting only touches their *sizes*.** Theorem 20.1 turns "positive definite" into a checklist on signs: all $\lambda_i>0$. Two things then follow almost for free. First, "$0$ is not on the list" is the same as "no direction gets crushed to nothing," which is exactly invertibility — a matrix fails to be invertible precisely when some nonzero $v$ lands on $0$, i.e. when $0$ is an eigenvalue. Second, $A^{-1}$ acts on the *same* eigen-directions as $A$ (it reuses each eigenvector $v$), only replacing each stretch factor $\lambda$ by its reciprocal $1/\lambda$. Reciprocating a positive number keeps it positive — it never flips sign — so the whole positive-sign checklist survives, and $A^{-1}$ is positive definite too.<br><br>**Step 1 — invertibility from "no zero eigenvalue."** All $\lambda_i>0$ (Theorem 20.1) $\Rightarrow$ $0\notin\{\lambda_i\}$ $\Rightarrow$ $N(A)=\{0\}$ (eigenvalue/null-space correspondence, Day 10/11) $\Rightarrow$ $A$ invertible. Why the middle step is legal: $0$ being an eigenvalue *means* $Av=0$ for some $v\neq0$, i.e. a nontrivial null space; forbidding $0$ forbids that.<br><br>**Step 2 — reciprocal eigenvalues, same eigenvector.** From $Av=\lambda v$, hit both sides with $A^{-1}$: $v=\lambda A^{-1}v$; divide by $\lambda\neq0$ to get $A^{-1}v=\tfrac{1}{\lambda}v$. So $A^{-1}$ inherits $A$'s eigenvectors and inverts its eigenvalues. Since every $\lambda_i>0$, every $1/\lambda_i>0$.<br><br>**Step 3 — $A^{-1}$ is still symmetric, so Theorem 20.1 applies to it.** $(A^{-1})^T=(A^T)^{-1}=A^{-1}$ uses $(M^T)^{-1}=(M^{-1})^T$ and $A^T=A$. Theorem 20.1 needs a *symmetric* matrix with all-positive eigenvalues; $A^{-1}$ meets both, so it is positive definite.<br><br>**What just happened:** positivity of every eigenvalue rules out the one bad value ($0$) that would block inversion, and inverting merely flips each positive stretch to another positive stretch on the same axes — so the positive-definite property passes straight to $A^{-1}$. |

### Exercise 9 — Positive definite $\Rightarrow$ every diagonal entry $A_{ii}>0$ (necessary, not sufficient)

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Let $A$ be positive definite and fix any index $i \in \{1,\dots,n\}$. Take $x = e_i$, the $i$-th standard basis vector, which is nonzero. Then $x^TAx = e_i^TAe_i = A_{ii}$ (this is a standard fact about how $e_i^TMe_i$ picks out the $(i,i)$ entry of any matrix $M$: $Me_i$ is the $i$-th column of $M$, and $e_i^T$ dotted with that column picks out its $i$-th entry, which is $M_{ii}$). Since $A$ is positive definite and $e_i \neq 0$, $x^TAx > 0$, i.e. $A_{ii} > 0$. Since $i$ was arbitrary, every diagonal entry of $A$ is strictly positive.<br><br>This does **not** contradict Exercise 8: positive diagonal entries are a *necessary* condition for positive definiteness (this exercise), not a *sufficient* one (Exercise 8 and the Unconventional edge section) — $A_8$ satisfies the necessary condition (both diagonal entries positive) while still failing to be positive definite, because the off-diagonal entry is too large relative to the diagonal. | **Intuition — the diagonal entry $A_{ii}$ is just the value of the form on the $i$-th coordinate axis.** Definiteness (Definition 20.2) demands $x^TAx>0$ for *every* nonzero $x$. The cheapest nonzero vectors to test are the axis vectors $e_1,\dots,e_n$, and testing $A$ on $e_i$ reads off exactly the diagonal entry $A_{ii}$. So "positive on each axis direction" — which any positive-definite form must satisfy — is literally "$A_{ii}>0$ for each $i$." That is why positive diagonal is *forced*. But the axes are only $n$ special directions out of a whole sphere's worth; passing the test on them says nothing about the diagonal or mixed directions in between, which is precisely why the condition is necessary but not sufficient.<br><br>**Step 1 — why $e_i^TAe_i=A_{ii}$.** $Ae_i$ is the $i$-th column of $A$; left-multiplying by $e_i^T$ dots that column with $e_i$, extracting its $i$-th entry, namely $A_{ii}$. (Same "$e_i$ selects a coordinate" fact used throughout the course.)<br><br>**Step 2 — apply the definition on this one nonzero vector.** $e_i\neq0$, so positive definiteness gives $e_i^TAe_i>0$; combined with Step 1, $A_{ii}>0$. Since $i$ was arbitrary, this holds for all $n$ diagonal entries.<br><br>**Why no contradiction with Exercise 8:** necessary $\neq$ sufficient. $A_8=\begin{pmatrix}1&5\\5&1\end{pmatrix}$ *passes* the axis tests ($A_{11}=A_{22}=1>0$) yet is indefinite, because it *fails* on an off-axis direction: at $x=(1,-1)$, $x^TA_8x = 1+2(5)(1)(-1)+1 = -8<0$. The large off-diagonal entry sabotages a direction the diagonal test never looked at.<br><br>**What just happened:** evaluating the form on each coordinate axis both proves positive diagonals are mandatory and shows exactly why they aren't enough — the test only inspects $n$ directions, and definiteness is a claim about all of them. |

### Exercise 10 — $Q(x)\ge\lambda_{\min}\|x\|^2$, boundedness of sublevel sets, and why they are ellipsoids

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| *Deriving the bound.* From equation $(\ast)$ in Theorem 20.1's proof, with $y = Q^Tx$, $Q(x) = x^TAx = \sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2$ where $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ are the eigenvalues of $A$. Since every $\lambda_i \ge \lambda_{\min}$ (by definition of $\lambda_{\min}$ as the smallest eigenvalue) and every $y_i^2 \ge 0$, $Q(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i y_i^2 \ge \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_{\min} y_i^2 = \lambda_{\min}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^2 = \lambda_{\min}\|y\|^2.$ Since $Q$ is orthogonal, it preserves norms (Day 17's corollary), so $\|y\| = \|Q^Tx\| = \|x\|$ ($Q^T$ is itself orthogonal, by Exercise 4(b) of Day 17 applied to $Q^T$, or directly since $(Q^T)^TQ^T = QQ^T=I$ when $Q$ is square orthogonal). So $Q(x) \ge \lambda_{\min}\|x\|^2$ for every $x$.<br><br>*Boundedness.* Let $x \in S_c$, i.e. $Q(x) \le c$. Combining with the bound just derived, $\lambda_{\min}\|x\|^2 \le Q(x) \le c \implies \|x\|^2 \le \frac{c}{\lambda_{\min}} \implies \|x\| \le \sqrt{\frac{c}{\lambda_{\min}}}$ (dividing by $\lambda_{\min}>0$ preserves the inequality direction, and $\lambda_{\min}>0$ since $A$ is positive definite). So every $x \in S_c$ lies within distance $\sqrt{c/\lambda_{\min}}$ of the origin: $S_c$ is contained in a ball of that radius, hence bounded.<br><br>*Why an ellipsoid (brief).* In the rotated coordinates $y=Q^Tx$, the condition $Q(x)\le c$ reads $\sum_i \lambda_iy_i^2 \le c$, i.e. $\sum_i y_i^2/(c/\lambda_i) \le 1$ — precisely the interior of a standard axis-aligned ellipsoid with semi-axis lengths $\sqrt{c/\lambda_i}$. Since $x = Qy$ is just an orthogonal change of coordinates (a rotation/reflection, which distorts no shape, only reorients it), $S_c$ in the original $x$-coordinates is that same ellipsoid, rotated by $Q$ — so $S_c$ is a (possibly rotated) bounded ellipsoid, not merely some unspecified bounded region. This is the same "diagonalize, then read off the answer in the eigenbasis, then rotate back" pattern Day 12 used to make matrix powers and difference equations tractable — here it makes the geometry of a quadratic form tractable instead. | **Intuition — replace every stretch factor by the weakest one to get a clean lower bound.** In the eigen-coordinates $y=Q^Tx$ the form is a weighted sum of squares $\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2$: each axis is stretched by its own eigenvalue. The direction that grows *slowest* is the one with the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}$. If you pessimistically pretend *every* axis grows that slowly, you can only shrink the value, giving $Q(x)\ge\lambda_{\min}\|y\|^2$; and because the rotation $Q$ preserves length, $\|y\|=\|x\|$, so $Q(x)\ge\lambda_{\min}\|x\|^2$ in the original coordinates. This says the bowl $Q$ rises *at least* as fast as the paraboloid $\lambda_{\min}\|x\|^2$ — so if you cap its height at $c$, you cap how far out you can be. That is boundedness.<br><br>**Step 1 — the bound, term by term.** $\lambda_i\ge\lambda_{\min}$ (definition of smallest eigenvalue) and $y_i^2\ge0$, so each term drops when $\lambda_i$ is lowered to $\lambda_{\min}$: $\sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2\ge\sum_i\lambda_{\min}y_i^2=\lambda_{\min}\|y\|^2$. The rewrite $y=Q^Tx$ and $Q(x)=\sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2$ is equation $(\ast)$ from Theorem 20.1's proof.<br><br>**Step 2 — length is coordinate-free.** $Q$ orthogonal $\Rightarrow$ it preserves norms (Day 17), and $Q^T$ is orthogonal too, so $\|y\|=\|Q^Tx\|=\|x\|$. Hence $Q(x)\ge\lambda_{\min}\|x\|^2$ for all $x$.<br><br>**Step 3 — cap the height, cap the radius.** For $x\in S_c$: $\lambda_{\min}\|x\|^2\le Q(x)\le c$; divide by $\lambda_{\min}>0$ (legal, and positive because $A$ is positive definite) to get $\|x\|\le\sqrt{c/\lambda_{\min}}$. So $S_c$ sits inside the ball of that radius — bounded.<br><br>**Why the radius is exactly the longest semi-axis.** In eigen-coordinates $S_c$ is $\sum_i y_i^2/(c/\lambda_i)\le1$, an ellipsoid with semi-axes $\sqrt{c/\lambda_i}$. The *softest* stretch $\lambda_{\min}$ gives the *longest* semi-axis $\sqrt{c/\lambda_{\min}}$ — precisely the bounding radius from Step 3. Rotating back by $x=Qy$ (a rigid motion that reorients but never distorts) leaves it an ellipsoid, just tilted.<br><br>**What just happened:** flattening every eigenvalue to the smallest turns the form into a plain paraboloid $\lambda_{\min}\|x\|^2$ it always dominates, so a height cap $c$ forces a radius cap $\sqrt{c/\lambda_{\min}}$ — and reading the un-flattened form axis by axis shows the capped region is exactly a (rotated) ellipsoid whose longest axis is that radius. |
