### Exercise 8 — $n$ distinct eigenvalues $\implies$ diagonalizable

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** Suppose $A$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$. Each $\lambda_i$ is a root of $p_A$, so $N(A-\lambda_iI) \ne \{0\}$: pick a nonzero eigenvector $v_i \in N(A-\lambda_iI)$ for each $i$. By Day 10's theorem, eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent, and $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ are (by hypothesis) distinct, so $v_1,\dots,v_n$ are linearly independent. That's $n$ linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ — i.e. $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors of $A$. By Theorem 11.2, $A$ is diagonalizable. $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition — the proof is a two-link relay.** The chain is: *distinct eigenvalues* $\Rightarrow$ *independent eigenvectors* (Day 10) $\Rightarrow$ *diagonalizable* (Theorem 11.2). Neither link is re-proved here; the whole exercise is just clicking the two together, which is why the argument is so short. <br> **Why each $\lambda_i$ actually owns an eigenvector.** Being an eigenvalue *means* being a root of $p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)$, and a root makes $A-\lambda_i I$ singular, so $N(A-\lambda_i I)\neq\{0\}$ — there is a genuine nonzero $v_i$ to pick. You get one eigenvector *per* eigenvalue, so $n$ distinct eigenvalues hand you $n$ eigenvectors. <br> **Why distinctness is the crucial hypothesis.** Day 10's theorem says eigenvectors for *distinct* eigenvalues can never be redundant — each new eigenvalue points its eigenvector in a genuinely new direction that the earlier ones cannot reproduce. So the $n$ chosen $v_i$ are automatically independent; distinctness does all the work of guaranteeing independence *for free*, with no multiplicity bookkeeping. <br> **Why that finishes it.** $n$ independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is condition (2) of Theorem 11.3 — exactly "$A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors" — which Theorem 11.2 converts into $A=PDP^{-1}$. (Note this is a *sufficient* condition only: repeated eigenvalues may still diagonalize, as with $I_n$ — see the Unconventional edge.) <br> **What just happened:** distinct eigenvalues force their eigenvectors to point in $n$ genuinely different directions, and $n$ independent eigenvectors is precisely what diagonalizability requires. |

### Exercise 10 — $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$ by induction

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **10.** *Base case* $k=0$: $A^0 = I$ and $PD^0P^{-1} = PIP^{-1}=PP^{-1}=I$ (since $D^0$ is the identity — every diagonal entry raised to the $0$-th power is $1$). So the claim holds for $k=0$. *Inductive step:* suppose $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$ for some $k\ge 0$. Then $A^{k+1} = A^k \cdot A = \left(PD^kP^{-1}\right)\left(PDP^{-1}\right) = PD^k\left(P^{-1}P\right)DP^{-1} = PD^k(I)DP^{-1} = P\left(D^kD\right)P^{-1} = PD^{k+1}P^{-1},$ using $D^kD = D^{k+1}$ (diagonal matrices multiply entrywise, so powers of the same diagonal matrix add exponents, exactly like scalars). By induction, $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$ for every integer $k \ge 0$. $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition — powers in the eigenbasis are trivial.** $A=PDP^{-1}$ (Definition 11.2) says $A$ is "$D$ seen in the wrong coordinates": $P^{-1}$ converts into the eigenbasis, $D$ acts, $P$ converts back. Raising $A$ to a power just repeats that round trip — and the return trip of one step ($P$) immediately cancels the outbound trip of the next ($P^{-1}$), so all the inner conversions collapse and only the *first* $P$ and *last* $P^{-1}$ survive. That collapse is the whole content of the proof. <br> **Why the middle telescopes.** In $\left(PD^kP^{-1}\right)\left(PDP^{-1}\right)$ the adjacent factors $P^{-1}P$ meet in the middle and equal $I$ (inverse times matrix, Day 9), so they vanish and leave $PD^k D P^{-1}$. This is exactly why we conjugate: the conjugation "undoes itself" between consecutive powers. <br> **Why $D^kD=D^{k+1}$ is the easy part.** A diagonal matrix acts on each coordinate independently, so multiplying two of them just multiplies matching diagonal entries — powers of one diagonal matrix add exponents entrywise, exactly like ordinary scalars ($\lambda_i^k\cdot\lambda_i=\lambda_i^{k+1}$). The hard operation (a matrix power of $A$) has been traded for an easy one (scalar powers of the $\lambda_i$). <br> **Why induction is the right frame.** The base case $k=0$ anchors it ($A^0=I=PIP^{-1}$, using $D^0=I$), and the step shows "true at $k$ $\Rightarrow$ true at $k+1$" by peeling off one factor of $A$; together they cover every $k\ge 0$. <br> **What just happened:** to raise $A$ to a power, switch to the eigenbasis once, raise the easy diagonal $D$ to that power, and switch back once — all the intermediate coordinate changes cancel. |
