### Exercise 1 — Prove $\{0\}$ is a subspace of any vector space $V$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **1.** $\{0\}$ contains $0$ by definition. Closure under addition: $0 + 0 = 0 \in \{0\}$. Closure under scalar multiplication: for any $c$, $c \cdot 0 = 0 \in \{0\}$. All three conditions hold, so $\{0\}$ is a subspace (the *trivial* or *zero subspace*). | **Intuition.** $\{0\}$ is the smallest set that could possibly be a subspace: it has exactly one element, the zero vector. Run the three-item checklist of Definition 1.2 and notice there is nothing that *could* fail — every operation on the only available vector just returns that same vector. <br> **Why each step is legal.** Condition 1 (contains $0$): satisfied because $0$ is literally the only element. <br> Condition 2 (closed under $+$): the only sum you can form is $0+0$, and by axiom 3 of Definition 1.1 (additive identity) $0+0=0$, which is back in $\{0\}$. <br> Condition 3 (closed under scaling): the only scalar multiples are $c\cdot 0$; for every $c$ this equals $0\in\{0\}$ (a basic consequence of the axioms — scaling the zero vector can only land on the zero vector). Since $\{0\}$ passes all three conditions, Definition 1.2 says it is a subspace. <br> **What just happened:** with only one vector around, every closure requirement is trivially met, so the one-point set $\{0\}$ is automatically a subspace. |

### Exercise 5 — Two subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^2$ whose union is not a subspace

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **5.** Let $W_1 = \operatorname{span}\{(1,0)\}$ and $W_2 = \operatorname{span}\{(1,1)\}$ (any two distinct lines through the origin work). $(1,0) \in W_1$ and $(1,1) \in W_2$, so both are in $W_1 \cup W_2$. Their sum is $(2,1)$. Is $(2,1) \in W_1$? $W_1 = \{(t,0): t\in\mathbb R\}$ requires the second coordinate to be $0$; it's $1$, so no. Is $(2,1) \in W_2$? $W_2 = \{(t,t)\}$ requires both coordinates equal; $2 \neq 1$, so no. So $(2,1) \notin W_1 \cup W_2$, and the union isn't closed under addition — not a subspace. | **Intuition.** Picture $W_1$ and $W_2$ as two distinct lines through the origin — an "X" shape. Their union is only those two lines, nothing in between. But adding a vector pointing along one line to a vector pointing along the *other* line produces a vector that points into the open region *between* the lines — off both of them. That is the whole reason a union of subspaces almost always fails: it collects the directions but not the combinations of directions, and Definition 1.2 demands closure under those combinations. <br> **Why the key step works.** $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$ point in genuinely different directions (neither is a scalar multiple of the other), so their sum $(2,1)$ inherits a nonzero component along each. That forces it off $W_1$ (second coordinate $\neq 0$) and off $W_2$ (coordinates unequal) simultaneously — exactly the witness that breaks closure under addition. <br> **What just happened:** gluing two lines together gives back the directions but not their sums, so the union misses $(1,0)+(1,1)=(2,1)$ and fails the closure test — not a subspace. |

### Exercise 6 — Is $\operatorname{span}(S)$ for $S=\{(1,2,0),(0,1,1),(1,3,1)\}$ all of $\mathbb{R}^3$, a plane, or a line?

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **6.** Check whether the third vector is redundant: $(1,2,0) + (0,1,1) = (1,3,1)$ — yes, exactly the third vector. So $\operatorname{span}(S) = \operatorname{span}\{(1,2,0),(0,1,1)\}$. These two remaining vectors are not scalar multiples of each other (so they're linearly independent — a formal test for this is Day 2's topic), so their span is a genuine 2-dimensional subspace: a **plane through the origin**, not all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ and not a line. | **Step 1 — verify the redundancy coordinatewise.** Add the first two vectors component by component: $(1,2,0)+(0,1,1)=(1+0,\,2+1,\,0+1)=(1,3,1)$. This equals the third vector exactly, so $(1,3,1)=1\cdot(1,2,0)+1\cdot(0,1,1)$ is a linear combination of the other two (Definition 1.3). <br> **Step 2 — why dropping it leaves the span unchanged.** Any combination $a(1,2,0)+b(0,1,1)+c(1,3,1)$ can rewrite $(1,3,1)$ as $(1,2,0)+(0,1,1)$, giving $(a+c)(1,2,0)+(b+c)(0,1,1)$ — still just a combination of the first two. Hence $\operatorname{span}(S)=\operatorname{span}\{(1,2,0),(0,1,1)\}$. <br> **Step 3 — the "not scalar multiples" check the original skips.** Suppose $(0,1,1)=k(1,2,0)$ for some scalar $k$. The first coordinate forces $0=k\cdot 1$, so $k=0$; but then $k(1,2,0)=(0,0,0)\neq(0,1,1)$ — contradiction. So no such $k$ exists, and neither vector is a scalar multiple of the other. <br> **Step 4 — conclude the dimension.** Two vectors that are not scalar multiples span a $2$-dimensional set, which is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$ by Theorem 1.1. A $2$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a plane through the origin — bigger than a line (one direction), smaller than all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ (three directions). <br> **What just happened:** the third vector was just the sum of the first two, so $S$ really only supplies two independent directions, and their span is a plane through the origin. |

### Exercise 8 — Prove $S_1 \subseteq S_2 \implies \operatorname{span}(S_1) \subseteq \operatorname{span}(S_2)$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** Let $w \in \operatorname{span}(S_1)$. Then $w = \sum_{i=1}^k a_i s_i$ for some $s_i \in S_1$. Since $S_1 \subseteq S_2$, each $s_i \in S_2$ as well, so this same expression exhibits $w$ as a finite linear combination of vectors in $S_2$, i.e. $w \in \operatorname{span}(S_2)$. Since $w$ was arbitrary, $\operatorname{span}(S_1) \subseteq \operatorname{span}(S_2)$. | **Intuition.** Think of a spanning set as a pantry of ingredients and the span as every dish you can cook from finitely many of them. If pantry $S_1$ sits inside pantry $S_2$, then any recipe you can make from $S_1$ you can still make from $S_2$ — every ingredient it calls for is still on the shelf. Nothing about the recipe changes; only the pantry got bigger. <br> **Why the key step works.** The proof never rebuilds $w$; it reuses the *same* combination $\sum a_i s_i$. The only thing that must be checked is that this expression still counts as "a combination of vectors from $S_2$", and that is exactly what $S_1\subseteq S_2$ guarantees: each $s_i\in S_1$ is also in $S_2$. By Definition 1.3, that makes $w\in\operatorname{span}(S_2)$. Because $w$ was an arbitrary element of $\operatorname{span}(S_1)$, the inclusion holds for the whole set. <br> **What just happened:** enlarging the ingredient set can only add recipes, never remove them, so a bigger spanning set has a bigger (or equal) span. |

### Exercise 9 — Prove $\operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S)) = \operatorname{span}(S)$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **9.** Since $S \subseteq \operatorname{span}(S)$ (every $s \in S$ equals $1 \cdot s \in \operatorname{span}(S)$), problem 8 gives $\operatorname{span}(S) \subseteq \operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S))$. For the reverse inclusion: by Theorem 1.1, $\operatorname{span}(S)$ is already a subspace, hence closed under addition and scalar multiplication; so *any* linear combination of vectors already in $\operatorname{span}(S)$ is again in $\operatorname{span}(S)$ (by repeated closure), i.e. $\operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S)) \subseteq \operatorname{span}(S)$. Both inclusions give equality. | **Intuition.** Spanning is a "closure" operation: it throws in every combination you were missing. Do it once and the set is already closed — do it a second time and there is nothing new left to add. So $\operatorname{span}$ applied twice equals $\operatorname{span}$ applied once (it is *idempotent*). The proof pins this down with two inclusions. <br> **Forward inclusion ($\subseteq$).** Every $s\in S$ is $1\cdot s$, so $S\subseteq\operatorname{span}(S)$; feeding this into Exercise 8 (monotonicity) gives $\operatorname{span}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S))$. This direction is automatic — spanning any set only makes it bigger. <br> **Reverse inclusion ($\supseteq$) — the real content.** An element of $\operatorname{span}(\operatorname{span}(S))$ is a combination of vectors that are *themselves* already in $\operatorname{span}(S)$. But Theorem 1.1 says $\operatorname{span}(S)$ is a subspace, so it is closed under addition and scaling; a combination of things already inside it stays inside it. So that element was in $\operatorname{span}(S)$ all along. <br> **What just happened:** once a set is closed under linear combinations, combining its elements yields nothing new — so spanning twice is the same as spanning once. |
