### Exercise 7 — if $A$ is invertible, so is $A^T$, with $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Since $A$ is invertible, $A^{-1}$ exists with $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$. Transpose both equations, using $(XY)^T = Y^TX^T$ and $I^T = I$: <br> $(AA^{-1})^T = (A^{-1})^TA^T = I, \qquad (A^{-1}A)^T = A^T(A^{-1})^T = I.$ <br> So $(A^{-1})^TA^T = A^T(A^{-1})^T = I$, which is exactly the defining condition (Definition 9.1) for $A^T$ to be invertible with inverse $(A^{-1})^T$. By uniqueness of the inverse (Theorem 9.1a), $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$. $\blacksquare$ | **The idea in one line.** We already *have* a matrix that undoes $A$; we just transpose the whole "undoing" equation and let the order-reversing rule for transposes ($(XY)^T = Y^TX^T$) do the work. <br> **Step 1 — start from what invertibility gives us.** By Definition 9.1, $A^{-1}$ satisfies both $AA^{-1} = I$ and $A^{-1}A = I$. These two equations are the raw material. <br> **Step 2 — transpose each equation.** Transposing preserves equality, so $(AA^{-1})^T = I^T$ and $(A^{-1}A)^T = I^T$. Now $I^T = I$ (the identity is symmetric), and the product-transpose rule flips the order of the factors: $(AA^{-1})^T = (A^{-1})^T A^T$ and $(A^{-1}A)^T = A^T (A^{-1})^T$. <br> **Step 3 — why order-flipping is exactly what we want.** The flip turns "$A^{-1}$ on the right of $A$" into "$(A^{-1})^T$ on the left of $A^T$." The two transposed equations read $(A^{-1})^T A^T = I$ and $A^T (A^{-1})^T = I$ — a matrix that multiplies $A^T$ to $I$ from both sides. <br> **Step 4 — match the definition and invoke uniqueness.** Those are precisely the two conditions in Definition 9.1 with $A^T$ in the role of the matrix and $(A^{-1})^T$ in the role of its inverse. So $A^T$ is invertible, and by uniqueness (Theorem 9.1a) that inverse is *the* inverse: $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$. <br> **What just happened:** transpose flips a product's order, which is exactly the swap needed to turn "$A^{-1}$ undoes $A$" into "$(A^{-1})^T$ undoes $A^T$." |

### Exercise 8 — if $A$ is invertible then so is $A^k$, with $(A^k)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^k$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Induction on $k$. *Base case* $k=1$: $A^1 = A$ is invertible with $(A^1)^{-1} = A^{-1} = (A^{-1})^1$, given. *Inductive step:* suppose $A^{k}$ is invertible with $(A^k)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^k$. Then $A^{k+1} = A^k \cdot A$ is a product of two invertible matrices ($A^k$ by the inductive hypothesis, $A$ by assumption), so by Theorem 9.1(b), $A^{k+1}$ is invertible with <br> $(A^{k+1})^{-1} = (A^k A)^{-1} = A^{-1}(A^k)^{-1} = A^{-1}(A^{-1})^k = (A^{-1})^{k+1}.$ <br> By induction, the claim holds for every positive integer $k$. $\blacksquare$ | **The idea in one line.** Theorem 9.1(b) already tells us a product of two invertibles is invertible and inverts in reverse order; raising $A$ to a power is just repeating that one fact, so induction bolts the powers together one at a time. <br> **Step 1 — base case is free.** For $k=1$, $A^1$ is literally $A$, which is invertible by hypothesis, and $(A^1)^{-1} = A^{-1} = (A^{-1})^1$. The formula holds trivially. <br> **Step 2 — the inductive hypothesis.** Assume for some $k$ that $A^k$ is invertible and $(A^k)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^k$. We want the same statement for $k+1$. <br> **Step 3 — split off one factor.** Write $A^{k+1} = A^k \cdot A$. Both pieces are invertible ($A^k$ by the hypothesis, $A$ by assumption), so Theorem 9.1(b) applies to this *two-factor* product and gives $(A^k A)^{-1} = A^{-1}(A^k)^{-1}$ — note the reverse order: the inverse of the *right* factor comes first. <br> **Step 4 — substitute the hypothesis and collect.** Replace $(A^k)^{-1}$ by $(A^{-1})^k$ (the inductive hypothesis): $A^{-1}(A^{-1})^k = (A^{-1})^{k+1}$, since $A^{-1}$ times $k$ more copies of $A^{-1}$ is $k+1$ copies. That is the claim for $k+1$. <br> **Why the reversal is invisible here.** Theorem 9.1(b) reverses the order of factors, but every factor is the *same* matrix $A^{-1}$, so "reversed order" and "original order" look identical — the bookkeeping simplifies to a clean power. <br> **What just happened:** each extra power of $A$ is undone by one extra power of $A^{-1}$, and induction chains that single step into $(A^k)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^k$ for all $k$. |
