### Exercise 7 — $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$ by induction on $n$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **7.** By induction on $n$. *Base case* $n=1$: $A^T = A$ trivially for a $1\times1$ matrix, so $\det(A^T)=\det(A)$. <br><br> *Inductive step.* Assume $\det(B^T) = \det(B)$ for all $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ matrices $B$. Let $A$ be $n\times n$. Expand $\det(A)$ along row $1$: $\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{1+j}a_{1j}\det(A_{(1,j)}).$ Now consider $A^T$. Column $1$ of $A^T$ equals row $1$ of $A$, i.e. entry $i$ of column $1$ of $A^T$ is $(A^T)_{i1} = a_{1i}$. Expand $\det(A^T)$ down column $1$: $\det(A^T) = \sum_{i=1}^n (-1)^{i+1}(A^T)_{i1}\det\big((A^T)_{(i,1)}\big) = \sum_{i=1}^n (-1)^{i+1}a_{1i}\det\big((A^T)_{(i,1)}\big).$ The minor $(A^T)_{(i,1)}$ (delete row $i$, column $1$ from $A^T$) is the transpose of the minor $A_{(1,i)}$ (delete row $1$, column $i$ from $A$): deleting row $i$/column $1$ of $A^T$ and then transposing back is the same set of entries as deleting row $1$/column $i$ of $A$, just transposed, since transposing swaps the roles of "row $1$" and "column $1$" and of "column $i$" and "row $i$". Both are $(n-1)\times(n-1)$, so by the inductive hypothesis $\det\big((A^T)_{(i,1)}\big) = \det\big((A_{(1,i)})^T\big) = \det(A_{(1,i)})$. Substituting (and relabeling the summation index $i$ as $j$): $\det(A^T) = \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{j+1}a_{1j}\det(A_{(1,j)}) = \det(A),$ matching the row-$1$ expansion of $\det(A)$ term for term (since $(-1)^{j+1}=(-1)^{1+j}$). $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition — why induction is the right tool.** Cofactor expansion (Definition 8.2) *builds* an $n\times n$ determinant out of $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ ones. So a claim about $\det$ that is true one size down will propagate up the recursion — which is exactly what structural induction formalizes. The base case is free: a $1\times1$ matrix literally equals its own transpose. <br> **The one real idea — a row of $A$ is a column of $A^T$.** Transposing is defined so that row $1$ of $A$ becomes column $1$ of $A^T$; that is why the solution expands $A$ *along row $1$* but $A^T$ *down column $1$* (Definition 8.2 guarantees both expansions equal the determinant). Comparing the two sums term by term: the coefficients match, because $(A^T)_{i1}=a_{1i}$ are the very same numbers; the signs match, because $(-1)^{i+j}$ is symmetric in $i$ and $j$, so $(-1)^{j+1}=(-1)^{1+j}$. <br> **Why the minors line up.** The only piece not immediately equal is the minor determinant, and this is where the induction hypothesis is spent: deleting-then-transposing and transposing-then-deleting give the same $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ matrix, so $(A^T)_{(i,1)} = (A_{(1,i)})^T$. Being one size smaller, the hypothesis says its determinant is unchanged by that transpose. With coefficients, signs, and minors all matching, the two sums are identical. <br> **What just happened:** the determinant is built out of rows and columns in a perfectly symmetric way, so swapping their roles (transposing) cannot change it — and induction lets that symmetry cascade from the trivial $1\times1$ case all the way up. |

### Exercise 8 — $\det(cA)$ in terms of $c$, $n$, $\det(A)$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** $\det(cA) = c^n\det(A)$, *not* $c\det(A)$. Reasoning: $cA$ is $A$ with *every one* of its $n$ columns (or rows) scaled by $c$. By multilinearity (property (a)), scaling one column by $c$ multiplies $\det$ by $c$; scaling all $n$ columns by $c$, one at a time, multiplies $\det$ by $c$ a total of $n$ times, i.e. by $c^n$. Sanity check with $n=2$, $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $c=2$: $\det(A)=1$, $2A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$, $\det(2A)=4=2^2\cdot1$ — matches $c^n\det(A)$, not $c\det(A)=2$. |  **Intuition — why the exponent is $n$, the dimension.** Read $\det$ as signed volume scaling (today's Unconventional edge). Multiplying $A$ by $c$ stretches space by a factor of $c$ along *each* of the $n$ independent coordinate directions at once. Volume responds to a stretch in every direction independently, so it picks up one factor of $c$ per direction — $n$ of them — giving $c^n$. It is the same reason a cube of side length scaled by $c$ has its volume scaled by $c^3$ in $3$D: the exponent counts dimensions. <br> **Why the tempting guess $c\det(A)$ is wrong.** That guess quietly assumes $\det$ is linear in the *whole* matrix. It is not — property (a) says $\det$ is *multi*linear, linear in each column *separately*. Pulling the scalar $c$ out of column $1$ leaves it still sitting inside the other $n-1$ columns; you must pull it out of every column in turn, and each pull contributes one factor $c$. One column gives $c$; all $n$ give $c^n$. <br> **Why the sanity check is worth doing.** The $n=2$ example makes the gap concrete: $\det(2I_2)=4=2^2$, decisively not $2$. Any time an exponent "feels" like it should be a plain coefficient, a $2\times2$ case is the cheapest way to catch the error. <br> **What just happened:** scaling a matrix by $c$ stretches all $n$ dimensions by $c$, and since the determinant measures $n$-dimensional volume, it scales by $c^n$ — one factor per dimension, not one factor total. |

### Exercise 10 — $A^2 = 0 \implies \det(A) = 0$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **10.** By Theorem 8.1, $\det(A)^2 = \det(A)\det(A) = \det(A\cdot A) = \det(A^2) = \det(0_{n\times n})$. The zero matrix has every row equal to the zero vector, so by Lemma 8.1, $\det(0_{n\times n}) = 0$. Hence $\det(A)^2 = 0$. Since $\det(A)$ is a real number and the only real number whose square is $0$ is $0$ itself, $\det(A) = 0$. $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition — what the argument is really doing.** The determinant (Theorem 8.1) is a *homomorphism*: it turns matrix multiplication into ordinary number multiplication. So the matrix fact "$A^2$ is the zero matrix" gets translated into the scalar fact "$\det(A)^2$ is the number $0$" — and scalars are far easier to reason about. Once you are looking at a real number whose square is $0$, there is only one possibility: the number itself is $0$. <br> **Why the pieces are legal.** $\det(A)^2 = \det(A^2)$ is Theorem 8.1 with $B=A$; $\det(0_{n\times n})=0$ is Lemma 8.1 (a matrix of all-zero rows has a zero row, in fact every row, so its determinant vanishes). <br> **The geometric why.** Suppose, for contrast, $A$ preserved some volume — i.e. $\det(A)\neq 0$, so $A$ is invertible (Theorem 8.2). Then $A^2$ would scale volume by $\det(A)^2\neq 0$ and so be invertible too — but $A^2=0$ is the *most* volume-destroying map there is. That contradiction means $A$ cannot have preserved any volume in the first place: it must already flatten space into a lower dimension, which is exactly $\det(A)=0$. <br> **What just happened:** if $A$ scaled volume by any nonzero amount, doing it twice would still leave nonzero volume, but $A^2$ crushes everything to zero — so $A$ must already be volume-destroying, i.e. $\det(A)=0$. |
