### Exercise 6 — Exponent law $A^{j+k} = A^jA^k$ for a diagonalizable $A$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| By Theorem 12.1, $A^j = PD^jP^{-1}$ and $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$. Then $A^jA^k = (PD^jP^{-1})(PD^kP^{-1}) = PD^j(P^{-1}P)D^kP^{-1} = PD^jD^kP^{-1}$ using $P^{-1}P=I$ and associativity, exactly as in the proof of Theorem 12.1. Since $D$ is diagonal, $D^jD^k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^j\lambda_1^k,\dots)=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^{j+k},\dots)=D^{j+k}$. So $A^jA^k = PD^{j+k}P^{-1} = A^{j+k}$ (again by Theorem 12.1, applied to exponent $j+k$). $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition — the whole argument is "push the exponent law down onto $D$, where it is free."** For a general matrix $A$, there is no reason exponents should add nicely — but $A = PDP^{-1}$ says $A$ is just the diagonal map $D$ *seen in different coordinates* ($P$ translates between the eigenbasis and the standard basis). Everything hard happens for $D$, and for a diagonal matrix the exponent law is obvious: scaling an axis by $\lambda^j$ then by $\lambda^k$ scales it by $\lambda^{j+k}$. <br> **Step 1 — write both powers via Theorem 12.1.** $A^j = PD^jP^{-1}$, $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$, so $A^jA^k = (PD^jP^{-1})(PD^kP^{-1})$. <br> **Step 2 — the inner $P^{-1}P$ collapses.** Regroup by associativity of matrix multiplication to get $PD^j(P^{-1}P)D^kP^{-1}$; since $P^{-1}P = I$ (definition of inverse), this is $PD^jD^kP^{-1}$. This is the *same* cancellation trick as in the proof of Theorem 12.1 — leaving the eigenbasis and immediately re-entering it does nothing. <br> **Step 3 — exponents add on the diagonal.** $D^jD^k$ multiplies corresponding diagonal entries: $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i^j\lambda_i^k) = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i^{j+k}) = D^{j+k}$, using the ordinary scalar law $\lambda_i^j\lambda_i^k=\lambda_i^{j+k}$ in each slot. <br> **Step 4 — repackage.** $A^jA^k = PD^{j+k}P^{-1}$, which by Theorem 12.1 (at exponent $j+k$) is exactly $A^{j+k}$. <br> **What just happened:** the exponent law survives for $A$ because conjugation by $P$ carries $A$ to a diagonal $D$, where adding exponents is just multiplying scalars slot by slot. |

### Exercise 7 — $|\lambda_i| < 1$ for all $i$ implies $A^k \to 0$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| Since $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$, $D^k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\dots,\lambda_n^k)$. For each $i$, $\vert \lambda_i\vert <1$ implies $\lambda_i^k \to 0$ as $k\to\infty$ (a standard scalar limit: $\vert \lambda_i^k\vert  = \vert \lambda_i\vert ^k \to 0$ since $\vert \lambda_i\vert <1$). So every diagonal entry of $D^k$ tends to $0$, i.e. $D^k \to 0$ (the zero matrix) entrywise. Now $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$ by Theorem 12.1, where $P$ and $P^{-1}$ are fixed matrices (their entries don't change with $k$). Each entry of $A^k$ is a fixed finite sum of products of entries of $P$, entries of $D^k$, and entries of $P^{-1}$; since the entries of $D^k$ all $\to 0$ and everything else is constant in $k$, each entry of $A^k$ is a finite sum of terms each tending to $0$, hence tends to $0$ itself. So $A^k \to 0$ entrywise, i.e. $A^k \to 0$ as $k\to\infty$. $\blacksquare$ | **Intuition — every eigendirection is a leaky bucket.** Applying $A$ once scales the $i$-th eigendirection by $\lambda_i$; applying it $k$ times scales it by $\lambda_i^k$. When $\vert \lambda_i\vert <1$, that direction shrinks geometrically toward $0$. A general vector is a combination of eigendirections, so *all* of it drains away — and "$A^k\to 0$" is just this statement made uniform across every input at once. The only part of $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$ that moves with $k$ is $D^k$; $P$ and $P^{-1}$ are a fixed change of coordinates that can't rescue a vector that has already collapsed to $0$ in the eigenbasis. <br> **Step 1 — reduce to the diagonal.** $D^k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\dots,\lambda_n^k)$, so controlling $A^k$ reduces to controlling each $\lambda_i^k$. <br> **Step 2 — scalar limit per entry.** $\vert \lambda_i^k\vert  = \vert \lambda_i\vert ^k$, and a real ratio $\vert \lambda_i\vert <1$ raised to higher powers tends to $0$; hence $\lambda_i^k\to 0$ and $D^k\to 0$ entrywise. <br> **Step 3 — the fixed frame can't undo the decay.** By Theorem 12.1, $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$ with $P,P^{-1}$ *independent of $k$*. Each entry of $A^k$ is a finite fixed sum of (entry of $P$)$\times$(entry of $D^k$)$\times$(entry of $P^{-1}$); with the $D^k$ factors $\to 0$ and the rest constant, every such entry $\to 0$. <br> **Why "finite sum" matters:** the number of terms in each entry is fixed by $n$ (it never grows with $k$), so "each term $\to 0$" safely gives "the sum $\to 0$" — a subtlety that would fail for an unbounded number of terms. <br> **What just happened:** shrinking each eigenvalue below magnitude $1$ makes $D^k$ vanish, and the fixed coordinate change $P(\cdot)P^{-1}$ passes that decay straight through to $A^k$. |

### Exercise 8 — Dominant eigenvalue governs long-run behavior; the Markov case

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| For large $n$, $x_n = A^nx_0 = PD^nP^{-1}x_0$ is dominated by the term involving $\lambda_{\max}^n$, since every other eigenvalue's power shrinks relative to it (this is why it's called "dominant"). (a) If $\vert \lambda_{\max}\vert >1$, that term grows without bound, so $\|x_n\|\to\infty$ — the sequence diverges (exponential growth), as in the Fibonacci sequence itself ($\varphi\approx1.618>1$). (b) If $\vert \lambda_{\max}\vert =1$, that term neither grows nor decays in magnitude, so $x_n$ settles toward a fixed nonzero limit (if $\lambda_{\max}=1$ exactly and it's the unique dominant eigenvalue) or oscillates with bounded magnitude (if $\lambda_{\max}=-1$ or complex with modulus 1) — no blow-up, no collapse to zero. (c) If $\vert \lambda_{\max}\vert <1$, then by Exercise 7's logic applied to the dominant term, $x_n \to 0$ — the sequence decays to the zero vector regardless of starting point. For a Markov chain's transition matrix, the dominant eigenvalue is always exactly $1$ (a structural fact about stochastic matrices — their rows/columns sum to 1, which forces $\lambda=1$ to be an eigenvalue and no eigenvalue to exceed it in magnitude). By case (b), the state vector $x_n = A^nx_0$ therefore neither blows up nor vanishes as $n\to\infty$: it converges to a fixed nonzero vector — the **stationary distribution** — which is exactly the eigenvector for $\lambda=1$ (normalized to sum to 1). This is the same $A^n = PD^nP^{-1}$ machinery from today, just with the extra structural guarantee $\vert \lambda_{\max}\vert =1$ baked in by what a Markov matrix *is*. | **Intuition — expand the start in the eigenbasis and watch a race.** Write $x_0 = c_1v_1 + \dots + c_nv_n$ in eigenvectors (this is what $P^{-1}x_0$ computes: the coordinates $c_i$). Then $x_n = A^nx_0 = c_1\lambda_1^n v_1 + \dots + c_n\lambda_n^n v_n$: each eigendirection evolves *independently*, scaled by its own $\lambda_i^n$. As $n$ grows this is a race between geometric sequences, and the largest $\vert \lambda_i\vert $ wins — factor it out, $x_n = \lambda_{\max}^n\big(c_{\max}v_{\max} + \sum_{i}c_i(\lambda_i/\lambda_{\max})^n v_i\big)$, and every ratio $\vert \lambda_i/\lambda_{\max}\vert <1$ decays to $0$ (Exercise 7's fact), leaving only the dominant term. So $\vert \lambda_{\max}\vert $ alone sets the overall scale. <br> **(a) $\vert \lambda_{\max}\vert >1$:** the surviving prefactor $\lambda_{\max}^n$ blows up, so $\|x_n\|\to\infty$ — pure exponential growth (Fibonacci, $\varphi\approx1.618$). <br> **(b) $\vert \lambda_{\max}\vert =1$:** the prefactor has constant magnitude, so $x_n$ stays bounded — it converges to $c_{\max}v_{\max}$ when $\lambda_{\max}=1$ is the unique dominant eigenvalue, or circles at bounded size when $\lambda_{\max}=-1$ or has modulus $1$. <br> **(c) $\vert \lambda_{\max}\vert <1$:** even the biggest term decays, so $x_n\to 0$ from any start — this is exactly Exercise 7 applied to the whole vector. <br> **Why Markov lands in case (b) on purpose:** a stochastic matrix has columns summing to $1$, which forces $\lambda=1$ to be an eigenvalue (the all-ones vector is a left eigenvector) and forbids any $\vert \lambda\vert >1$ (probability can't be created), so $\vert \lambda_{\max}\vert =1$ is *structural*, not a coincidence. Case (b) then says the probability vector neither explodes nor dies: it converges to the $\lambda=1$ eigenvector, normalized to sum to $1$ — the **stationary distribution**. <br> **What just happened:** in the eigenbasis a recurrence is $n$ independent geometric sequences, the largest-magnitude eigenvalue outruns the rest, and its size (bigger, smaller, or exactly $1$) decides whether the state grows, decays, or settles — with Markov matrices rigged so it is always exactly $1$. |
