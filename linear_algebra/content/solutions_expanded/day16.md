### Exercise 7 — Projecting a vector already in $W$ gives back the vector

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **7.** Since $e_1,\dots,e_k$ is a basis for $W$ and $v \in W$, write $v = \sum_{i=1}^k c_ie_i$ for some scalars $c_i$. By orthonormality, $\langle v,e_j\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = c_j$ (only the $i=j$ term survives). So $\operatorname{proj}_W(v) = \sum_{j=1}^k \langle v,e_j\rangle e_j = \sum_{j=1}^k c_je_j = v.$ | **Intuition.** Projection is "find the closest point of $W$ to $v$" (Theorem 16.1). If $v$ already lives in $W$, the closest point of $W$ to $v$ is $v$ itself — distance $0$ — so the answer has to be $v$. The computation below is just the Definition-16.1 formula confirming that. <br> **Step 1 — why $v$ is a combination of the $e_i$.** $e_1,\dots,e_k$ is a *basis* of $W$ and $v \in W$, so $v = \sum_{i=1}^k c_ie_i$ for unique scalars $c_i$. <br> **Step 2 — the projection reads off exactly those coordinates.** The key move is $\langle v,e_j\rangle = \sum_i c_i\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = c_j$: orthonormality means $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle$ is $1$ when $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise, so the whole sum collapses to the single term $c_j$. That is precisely why an orthonormal basis is so convenient — the $j$-th coordinate of $v$ is just $\langle v,e_j\rangle$, no linear system to solve. <br> **Step 3 — reassemble.** Plug into Definition 16.1: $\operatorname{proj}_W(v)=\sum_j\langle v,e_j\rangle e_j=\sum_j c_je_j=v$. <br> **What just happened:** projecting only moves a vector when it sticks out of $W$; a vector already inside $W$ is its own closest point, so projection leaves it fixed. |

### Exercise 8 — The residual is orthogonal to every column of $A$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **8.** From $A^TA\hat x = A^Tb$, rearrange to $A^T(A\hat x - b) = 0$, i.e. $A^T(b - A\hat x) = 0$ (the same equation up to an overall sign). Writing out $A^T$'s rows as the columns $a_1,\dots,a_n$ of $A$ (so row $j$ of $A^T$ is $a_j^T$), this single matrix equation says, row by row, $a_j^T(b - A\hat x) = 0, \quad \text{i.e.} \quad a_j \cdot (b-A\hat x) = 0, \qquad j = 1,\dots,n.$ So the residual $b - A\hat x$ is orthogonal to every column $a_j$ of $A$. | **Intuition.** The normal equations are *defined* as $A^TA\hat x=A^Tb$; this exercise asks you to read what that one matrix equation is actually saying, without invoking the geometry of Theorem 16.2. The trick is that "$A^T$ times a vector" packages $n$ separate dot products — one per column of $A$ — into a single column. Setting that column to zero forces all $n$ dot products to vanish at once. <br> **Step 1 — regroup into the residual.** Subtract to get $A^T(A\hat x-b)=0$; multiplying by $-1$ gives $A^T(b-A\hat x)=0$, the same statement written with the residual $r=b-A\hat x$. <br> **Step 2 — why the rows of $A^T$ are the columns of $A$.** Transposing turns column $a_j$ of $A$ into row $j$ of $A^T$. So the $j$-th entry of the product $A^T r$ is exactly $a_j^T r = a_j\cdot r$ (a dot product). <br> **Step 3 — a zero vector means every entry is zero.** $A^T r=0$ says each of its $n$ entries is $0$, i.e. $a_j\cdot(b-A\hat x)=0$ for every $j=1,\dots,n$. That is the definition of the residual being orthogonal to each column. <br> **What just happened:** the normal equations are, entry by entry, the single geometric demand that the leftover $b-A\hat x$ point straight out of the column space — perpendicular to every column of $A$ — recovered here by pure algebra. |

### Exercise 9 — $\hat x$ need not be unique, yet $A\hat x$ always is

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **9.** *Uniqueness of $\hat x$:* For any $x$, $x^TA^TAx = (Ax)^T(Ax) = \|Ax\|^2$. So $A^TAx = 0 \implies x^TA^TAx = 0 \implies \|Ax\|^2 = 0 \implies Ax = 0$; conversely $Ax=0 \implies A^TAx = A^T0 = 0$. Hence $N(A^TA) = N(A)$. If the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent, $N(A) \neq \{0\}$ (some nontrivial combination of columns vanishes), so $N(A^TA) \neq \{0\}$ too, meaning $A^TA$ is *not* invertible (a square matrix with nontrivial null space isn't invertible — Day 9). Consequently, if $\hat x_0$ solves the normal equations, so does $\hat x_0 + z$ for any $z \in N(A) = N(A^TA)$, since $A^TA(\hat x_0+z) = A^TA\hat x_0 + A^TAz = A^Tb + 0 = A^Tb$. Since $N(A) \neq \{0\}$ here, there are infinitely many solutions $\hat x$ — it is **not unique**. <br><br> *Uniqueness of $A\hat x$:* Yes, $A\hat x$ is still unique, regardless. This doesn't contradict the above because $A\hat x = \operatorname{proj}_{C(A)}(b)$ is characterized purely as "the closest point of the subspace $C(A)$ to $b$," which the Best Approximation Theorem (Theorem 16.1) guarantees is a *single, unique vector* no matter how many different coefficient vectors $\hat x$ happen to produce it. Linear dependence among $A$'s columns means several different $\hat x$'s describe the *same point* $A\hat x \in C(A)$ as a combination of those columns — a redundancy in the representation, not in the geometric answer. | **Intuition.** Separate two questions that feel like one: *where* is the best-fit point (a point in space), and *which coordinates* name it (a coefficient vector). Least squares always pins down the point; it only pins down the coordinates when the columns of $A$ give each point a single name. <br> **Step 1 — the identity that links $A^TA$ and $A$.** $x^TA^TAx=(Ax)^T(Ax)=\|Ax\|^2$ turns an algebraic quantity into a squared length. Since a length is zero only for the zero vector (positive-definiteness of the dot product), $A^TAx=0\Rightarrow\|Ax\|^2=0\Rightarrow Ax=0$. The reverse is immediate ($Ax=0\Rightarrow A^TAx=0$). So $N(A^TA)=N(A)$ — the two matrices have exactly the same null space even though $A^TA$ is the smaller $n\times n$ one. <br> **Step 2 — dependent columns kill invertibility.** Linearly dependent columns means some nonzero combination gives $Ax=0$, so $N(A)\neq\{0\}$, hence $N(A^TA)\neq\{0\}$; a square matrix with a nontrivial null space is not invertible (Day 9). <br> **Step 3 — a whole family of solutions.** If $\hat x_0$ solves the normal equations, so does $\hat x_0+z$ for every $z\in N(A^TA)$, because $A^TA(\hat x_0+z)=A^Tb+0=A^Tb$. With $N(A)\neq\{0\}$ that is infinitely many $\hat x$. <br> **Step 4 — why the point survives.** Every such $\hat x$ gives $A(\hat x_0+z)=A\hat x_0+Az=A\hat x_0$ (since $z\in N(A)$), the *same* vector. Theorem 16.1 already told us the closest point $\operatorname{proj}_{C(A)}(b)$ is unique; the different $\hat x$'s are just redundant recipes for that one point. <br> **What just happened:** when $A$'s columns are dependent they name the projected point in infinitely many ways, so $\hat x$ is not unique — but the geometric target $A\hat x$ is a single point fixed by the Best Approximation Theorem, so there is no contradiction. |

### Exercise 10 — Orthogonal projection is a linear map

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **10.** By Definition 16.1 and bilinearity of the inner product in its first argument: $\operatorname{proj}_W(au+bv) = \sum_{i=1}^k \langle au+bv, e_i\rangle e_i = \sum_{i=1}^k \big(a\langle u,e_i\rangle + b\langle v,e_i\rangle\big) e_i$ $= a\sum_{i=1}^k \langle u,e_i\rangle e_i + b\sum_{i=1}^k \langle v,e_i \rangle e_i = a\operatorname{proj}_W(u) + b\operatorname{proj}_W(v).$ | **Intuition.** Definition 16.1 builds the projection out of the coordinates $\langle v,e_i\rangle$. Each coordinate is an inner product, and inner products are linear in their first slot — so the coordinates of a combination are the same combination of the coordinates. Linearity of projection is nothing more than that linearity, carried through the sum. <br> **Step 1 — apply the definition to the input $au+bv$.** $\operatorname{proj}_W(au+bv)=\sum_i\langle au+bv,e_i\rangle e_i$. <br> **Step 2 — split each coordinate.** By bilinearity in the first argument, $\langle au+bv,e_i\rangle=a\langle u,e_i\rangle+b\langle v,e_i\rangle$. This is the only non-trivial step, and it is exactly the property of the inner product that Definition 16.1 relies on. <br> **Step 3 — regroup the sum.** A finite sum splits over its two pieces and the constants $a,b$ pull out: $\sum_i(a\langle u,e_i\rangle+b\langle v,e_i\rangle)e_i=a\sum_i\langle u,e_i\rangle e_i+b\sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i$. <br> **Step 4 — recognize the two projections.** Each remaining sum is Definition 16.1 again, so the expression is $a\operatorname{proj}_W(u)+b\operatorname{proj}_W(v)$. <br> **What just happened:** because projection reads off coordinates via inner products, and inner products respect linear combinations, projecting a combination equals combining the projections — so $\operatorname{proj}_W$ is a genuine linear map (and thus has a matrix, the theme Day 17 picks up). |
