### Exercise 5 — Equality in Cauchy-Schwarz $\iff$ $u,v$ linearly dependent

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| ($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $u,v$ are linearly dependent. If $v=0$, then (as shown in the proof of Theorem 14.1) both sides of Cauchy-Schwarz are $0$, so equality holds trivially, and $\{u,0\}$ is always a dependent set ($1\cdot 0 = 0$ is a nontrivial relation with a zero coefficient on $u$ — more precisely, $0\cdot u + 1 \cdot 0 = 0$ is a nontrivial dependence relation). If $v \neq 0$ and $u,v$ dependent, then $u = cv$ for some scalar $c$ (this is what dependence of a pair with $v\ne0$ means). Then $\langle u,v\rangle = \langle cv,v\rangle = c\langle v,v\rangle = c\|v\|^2$, so $\lvert\langle u,v\rangle\rvert = \lvert c\rvert\,\|v\|^2$. Also $\|u\| = \|cv\| = \sqrt{\langle cv,cv\rangle} = \sqrt{c^2\langle v,v\rangle} = \lvert c\rvert\,\|v\|$, so $\|u\|\|v\| = \lvert c\rvert\,\|v\|^2$ as well. Hence $\lvert\langle u,v\rangle\rvert = \|u\|\|v\|$: equality holds. <br><br> ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose equality holds: $\lvert\langle u,v\rangle\rvert = \|u\|\|v\|$. If $v = 0$, then $\{u,v\}$ is dependent as noted above, and we're done. If $v \neq 0$, revisit the proof of Theorem 14.1: $f(t) = \|u-tv\|^2 = At^2+Bt+C \ge 0$ for all $t$, with $A=\|v\|^2>0$, $B=-2\langle u,v\rangle$, $C=\|u\|^2$, and we showed $B^2-4AC \le 0$ is equivalent to Cauchy-Schwarz. Equality $\lvert\langle u,v\rangle\rvert = \|u\|\|v\|$ is exactly the case $B^2 - 4AC = 0$ (tracing back through the same algebra: $B^2-4AC = 4\langle u,v\rangle^2 - 4\|u\|^2\|v\|^2$, which is $0$ exactly when $\langle u,v\rangle^2 = \|u\|^2\|v\|^2$, i.e. $\lvert\langle u,v\rangle\rvert = \|u\|\|v\|$). A quadratic with $A>0$ and discriminant exactly $0$ has a *real double root* $t_0 = -B/(2A)$, and at that root the completed-square form shows $f(t_0) = 0$ exactly (the minimum value $C - B^2/(4A)$ equals $0$ when $B^2=4AC$). So $\|u - t_0v\|^2 = 0$, and by positive-definiteness (Definition 14.1, axiom 3), $u - t_0 v = 0$, i.e. $u = t_0 v$. This exhibits $u$ as a scalar multiple of $v$, so $u,v$ are linearly dependent. <br><br> Both directions hold, so equality in Cauchy-Schwarz holds if and only if $u,v$ are linearly dependent. | **The big picture.** Cauchy-Schwarz came from squeezing the parabola $f(t)=\|u-tv\|^2$ down toward the $t$-axis: it can never go below zero, so its discriminant is $\le 0$. The *inequality* is slack whenever the parabola stays strictly above the axis. Equality is the boundary case — the parabola just kisses the axis at one point. This exercise reads off what "just kisses" means geometrically: at that touch point $t_0$, $f(t_0)=\|u-t_0v\|^2=0$, i.e. $u-t_0v$ is the zero vector, i.e. $u$ lies exactly on the line through $v$. So the two conditions are the same fact seen two ways: "the parabola touches the axis" and "$u$ is a multiple of $v$." <br><br> **Why the forward direction is really just the reverse of the CS proof.** In Theorem 14.1 we threw away information: we only needed the *minimum* of $f$ to be $\ge 0$, giving discriminant $\le 0$. Here we keep track of *when* that minimum is exactly $0$. Discriminant $=0$ $\iff$ the completed square $A(t-t_0)^2 + (C-\tfrac{B^2}{4A})$ has vanishing constant part $\iff$ the parabola's lowest value is $0$ $\iff$ $f(t_0)=0$. Positive-definiteness (axiom 3) is what converts $\|u-t_0v\|^2=0$ into the vector equation $u-t_0v=0$ — a norm-squared of zero forces the vector itself to be zero. That single axiom is the whole engine of the $(\Rightarrow)$ direction. <br><br> **Why the easy direction is easy.** If $u=cv$, both $\langle u,v\rangle$ and $\|u\|\|v\|$ pick up the same factor $\lvert c\rvert\,\|v\|^2$, because scaling a vector scales its length by $\lvert c\rvert$ (the absolute value appears because $\|cv\|=\sqrt{c^2}\,\|v\|=\lvert c\rvert\,\|v\|$, and squaring erased the sign). Aligned vectors leave no room for slack — the projection of $u$ onto $v$ *is* all of $u$. <br><br> **What just happened:** equality in Cauchy-Schwarz means the parabola $\|u-tv\|^2$ actually reaches zero, and positive-definiteness turns that single zero into the statement that $u$ sits exactly on the line spanned by $v$ — the vectors are parallel.  |
