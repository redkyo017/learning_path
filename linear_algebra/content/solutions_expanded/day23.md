### Exercise 4 — Why centering is required (which step of Theorem 23.1 breaks)

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **4.** The break is in Step 1 of Theorem 23.1's proof: if $X$'s columns are not mean-zero, then $Xw = \sum_j w_jx_{(j)}$ is a linear combination of vectors that are *not* individually mean-zero, so $Xw$ itself generally has nonzero mean $\overline{Xw} = \sum_jw_j\bar x_{(j)} \ne 0$. Step 2's formula $\operatorname{Var}(v) = \frac1{n-1}\|v\|^2$ specifically used $\bar v = 0$ to drop the $(v_i-\bar v)$ down to $v_i$; for $\bar v \ne 0$ the correct formula is $\operatorname{Var}(v) = \frac1{n-1}\sum_i(v_i-\bar v)^2 = \frac1{n-1}\left(\sum_iv_i^2 - n\bar v^2\right) = \frac1{n-1}\|v\|^2 - \frac{n}{n-1}\bar v^2$ (expanding the square and using $\sum_i(v_i-\bar v)^2 = \sum_iv_i^2 - 2\bar v\sum_iv_i + n\bar v^2 = \sum_iv_i^2 - n\bar v^2$, since $\sum_iv_i = n\bar v$). So without centering, $\frac1{n-1}\|Xw\|^2 = \operatorname{Var}(Xw) + \frac{n}{n-1}\overline{Xw}^2 \ne \operatorname{Var}(Xw)$ in general — it's variance *plus* a penalty-free bonus term that grows with how far the projected mean is from $0$. Maximizing $w^TCw$ on uncentered data would therefore actually be maximizing "mean-square distance from the origin along direction $w$," which is dominated by wherever the data's centroid happens to sit relative to the origin — a direction that can be completely unrelated to how the data is actually spread out. Centering first removes this origin-dependent artifact so that what's maximized is genuine spread, not an accident of where the coordinate origin was placed. | **Intuition.** Two different things get quietly conflated: *variance* measures spread about the data's own mean, while $\frac1{n-1}\|Xw\|^2$ measures spread about the *origin*. They are the same number only when the mean already sits at the origin — which is exactly what centering arranges. Track the identity $w^TCw=\frac1{n-1}\|Xw\|^2$ from Theorem 23.1: it silently assumes the mean is zero, and that assumption is what fails. <br> **Step 1 — locate the broken assumption.** Step 1 of Theorem 23.1 concluded $Xw$ is mean-zero *because* every column of $X$ was. Drop centering and that collapses: $\overline{Xw}=\sum_j w_j\bar x_{(j)}\ne 0$. Everything downstream that used $\bar v=0$ is now invalid. <br> **Step 2 — the honest variance formula.** For general $\bar v$, $\operatorname{Var}(v)=\frac1{n-1}\sum_i(v_i-\bar v)^2$. Expanding the square and using $\sum_i v_i=n\bar v$ gives $\sum_i(v_i-\bar v)^2=\|v\|^2-n\bar v^2$, so $\operatorname{Var}(v)=\frac1{n-1}\|v\|^2-\frac{n}{n-1}\bar v^2$. The raw squared length overcounts variance by exactly $\frac{n}{n-1}\bar v^2$ — the offset of the data's center from the origin. <br> **Step 3 — what you'd actually be maximizing.** With $v=Xw$, $\frac1{n-1}\|Xw\|^2=\operatorname{Var}(Xw)+\frac{n}{n-1}\overline{Xw}^2$. Maximizing $w^TCw$ now chases mean-square distance from the origin, so $w$ tips toward wherever the centroid happens to lie — an artifact of coordinate placement, not of the cloud's shape. <br> **What just happened:** centering slides the cloud so its center sits on the origin; then "distance from the origin" and "spread about the mean" coincide, and $w^TCw$ genuinely measures spread — otherwise PCA would just point at the centroid. |

### Exercise 5 — $\operatorname{trace}(C)$ equals total variance equals $\sum_i\lambda_i$

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **5.** *First equality.* By definition, $C_{ii} = \frac1{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^n X_{ki}^2$ (the diagonal entries of $\frac1{n-1}X^TX$ are dot products of a column with itself). Since column $i$, $x_{(i)}$, is centered (mean $0$), Step 2 of Theorem 23.1's proof gives $\operatorname{Var}(x_{(i)}) = \frac1{n-1}\|x_{(i)}\|^2 = \frac1{n-1}\sum_kX_{ki}^2 = C_{ii}$. Summing over $i$: $\operatorname{trace}(C) = \sum_iC_{ii} = \sum_i\operatorname{Var}(x_{(i)})$. <br><br> *Second equality.* By the Spectral Theorem, $C = Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $Q$ orthogonal ($Q^TQ=QQ^T=I$) and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots,\lambda_p)$. Using the cyclic property of trace, $\operatorname{trace}(ABC)=\operatorname{trace}(BCA)$: $\operatorname{trace}(C) = \operatorname{trace}(Q\Lambda Q^T) = \operatorname{trace}(\Lambda Q^TQ) = \operatorname{trace}(\Lambda I) = \operatorname{trace}(\Lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^p\lambda_i.$ Combining both equalities: $\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(x_{(i)}) = \operatorname{trace}(C) = \sum_i\lambda_i$. So "total variance" can be read directly off the raw data (sum the per-feature variances, no eigendecomposition needed) or off the eigenvalues (sum $C$'s eigenvalues) — both routes must agree, and this is exactly the denominator $\sum_j\lambda_j$ used in the explained variance ratio (Definition 23.2). | **Intuition.** "Total variance" is a single physical quantity — how spread the cloud is, added up over all directions — and it should not care what coordinate system you measure it in. The diagonal of $C$ reports it in the *original feature* coordinates; the eigenvalues report it in the *principal-component* coordinates $q_1,\dots,q_p$. The eigendecomposition just rotates from one orthonormal frame to the other, and a rotation can neither create nor destroy spread, so the two sums are forced to agree. The cyclic-trace step is the algebraic shadow of that "rotation preserves total variance." <br> **Step 1 — diagonal of $C$ = per-feature variances.** $C_{ii}=\frac1{n-1}\sum_k X_{ki}^2$ is column $i$ dotted with itself; centering (Theorem 23.1, Step 2) makes that $\operatorname{Var}(x_{(i)})$. Sum down the diagonal: $\operatorname{trace}(C)=\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(x_{(i)})$. <br> **Step 2 — eigenvalues via the cyclic trace.** Write $C=Q\Lambda Q^T$ (Spectral Theorem). The only real move is $\operatorname{trace}(Q\Lambda Q^T)=\operatorname{trace}(\Lambda Q^TQ)$ — cycling $Q$ to the back — after which $Q^TQ=I$ collapses everything to $\operatorname{trace}(\Lambda)=\sum_i\lambda_i$. Why the cyclic law is legal: $\operatorname{trace}(AB)=\operatorname{trace}(BA)$ always, and here it turns the orthogonal $Q$ into $I$. <br> **Step 3 — equate the two readings.** Both expressions equal $\operatorname{trace}(C)$, so $\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(x_{(i)})=\sum_i\lambda_i$. This is the denominator in $\text{EVR}_k=\lambda_k/\sum_j\lambda_j$ (Definition 23.2). <br> **What just happened:** trace is rotation-invariant, so summing variances feature-by-feature and summing the eigenvalues are two views of the same total spread — which is why you can compute PCA's denominator either way and why the explained-variance ratios always add to $1$. |

### Exercise 7 — The unit-scaling pitfall and the correlation-matrix fix

| Original | Expanded |
|---|---|
| **7.** Mean is already $(0,0)$. $X^TX$: $(1,1)$ entry $=10^2\cdot4=400$; $(2,2)$ entry $=0.1^2\cdot4=0.04$; $(1,2)$ entry $=10(0.1)+(-10)(0.1)+10(-0.1)+(-10)(-0.1)=1-1-1+1=0$. So $C=\frac14\begin{pmatrix}400&0\\0&0.04\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}100&0\\0&0.01\end{pmatrix}.$ Since $C$ is already diagonal, its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries, $\lambda_1=100$ (eigenvector $(1,0)$, feature 1's axis) and $\lambda_2=0.01$ (eigenvector $(0,1)$, feature 2's axis) — PC1 is feature 1 (millimeters), overwhelmingly, purely because millimeter-scale numbers happen to be numerically larger than the kilometer-scale numbers, with **zero** actual correlation between the features driving this (the off-diagonal entry is exactly $0$). If feature 1 were instead expressed in kilometers too (dividing every value by $1{,}000{,}000$), the new $(1,1)$ entry of $C$ would shrink to $100/10^{12}=10^{-10} \ll 0.01$, and PC1 would instead point along the *old* feature 2's axis — the identity of "PC1" flips entirely based on an arbitrary choice of measurement units, even though nothing about the actual data changed. This shows PCA run on the raw covariance matrix is not unit-invariant, and is exactly why practitioners standardize each feature to unit variance first (subtract the mean *and* divide by the standard deviation) before running PCA whenever features are on incomparable scales; doing so replaces $C$ with the **correlation matrix** (the covariance matrix of the standardized features, which by construction has $1$'s on the diagonal), so no single feature can dominate PC1 merely by virtue of its units. | **Intuition.** A covariance entry carries units — $C_{11}$ is in mm$^2$, $C_{22}$ is in km$^2$ — so comparing them to decide "which direction has more variance" compares numbers in *incommensurable* units. Whichever feature is written in the smaller unit wins by default, purely because small units make numbers large. Theorem 23.3 is still perfectly true; the problem is that the matrix $C$ you feed it encodes an arbitrary unit convention, so its top eigenvector reflects that convention rather than the data. <br> **Step 1 — read PC1 off the diagonal.** With mean already $(0,0)$, $C=\operatorname{diag}(100,\,0.01)$ is diagonal, so its eigenvectors are the axes and its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries: $\lambda_1=100$ along $(1,0)$, $\lambda_2=0.01$ along $(0,1)$. PC1 is feature 1 — and crucially the off-diagonal is exactly $0$, so no genuine feature *correlation* drives this; it is pure scale. <br> **Step 2 — change one unit and watch PC1 flip.** Rewrite feature 1 in kilometers (divide values by $10^6$); every $x$-value shrinks by $10^6$, so $C_{11}$ shrinks by $10^{12}$ to $100/10^{12}=10^{-10}$. Now $10^{-10}\ll 0.01$, so $\lambda_1$ is the *other* axis and PC1 jumps to old feature 2. The data cloud never moved — only the ruler did. <br> **Step 3 — the fix.** Standardize each feature (subtract mean, divide by its standard deviation) before PCA. This rescales every feature to variance $1$, so $C$ becomes the **correlation matrix** with $1$'s on the diagonal; no feature can dominate through units alone, and only real cross-feature correlation shapes the components. <br> **What just happened:** raw-covariance PCA is not unit-invariant — rescale one feature and PC1 can swing 90° — so when features live on unlike scales you standardize first, which is precisely running PCA on the correlation matrix instead of the covariance matrix. |
