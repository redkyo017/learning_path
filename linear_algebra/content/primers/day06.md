## The hook

Suppose you have a matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6\end{pmatrix}$ (2 rows, 3 columns). Row-reducing: $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$ gives $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, so rank $r=1$.

Here's the magic: **four subspaces are hiding inside** this one matrix—and they're all visible through the lens of that rank-1 structure. Each subspace tells a different story about what $A$ does.

The **column space** $C(A)$ is what $A$ can produce. Every output $Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$. Since row 2 is a multiple of row 1, only column 1 survives as independent; columns 2 and 3 are dependent on column 1. So $C(A)$ is the line through $(1,2)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$: all scalar multiples of $\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}$. Try it: if you want to reach $(2,4)$, you compute $A\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\4\end{pmatrix}$. The output is always on this line.

The **null space** $N(A)$ is what $A$ silences (what maps to zero). Solving $Ax = 0$ gives $x_1 + 2x_2 + 3x_3 = 0$, so $x_1 = -2x_2 - 3x_3$ with $x_2, x_3$ free. The null space is a 2-dimensional plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$, spanned by $\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}-3\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$. Any vector in this plane gets sent to the zero vector.

The **row space** $C(A^T)$ is the span of the rows of $A$. Since row 2 is exactly $2 \times$ row 1, the row space is 1-dimensional, generated by the row $(1,2,3)$. It's a line in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

The **left null space** $N(A^T)$ is what $A^T$ silences, or equivalently, all $y$ where the rows of $A$ vanish when multiplied by $y$. Since row 2 is $2 \times$ row 1, we have $y = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}$ works: check $1 \cdot (1,2,3) - 2 \cdot (2,4,6) = (0,0,0)$. It's a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Four subspaces, one rank. Both row and column spaces have dimension $r=1$. The null space is $3-1=2$ dimensions. The left null space is $2-1=1$ dimension. The dimensions $\{1, 1, 2, 1\}$ all flow from the single number $r=1$. This is no accident—it's Theorem 6.1.

## The pictures

**Picture 1: The Four Rooms (Fundamental Subspace Partition).**  
Draw two columns standing side by side. On the left (domain $\mathbb{R}^n$), draw a vertical line splitting it into two regions: "row space" (dimension $r$) on top, "null space" (dimension $n-r$) on bottom. On the right (codomain $\mathbb{R}^m$), draw a vertical line splitting it: "column space" (dimension $r$) on top, "left null space" (dimension $m-r$) on bottom. Now draw arrows from left to right: from the row-space room to the column-space room (labeled $A$: "carries here"), and from the null-space room to the origin/zero vector (labeled $A$: "kills everything here"). The two regions on each side sit *side-by-side*, not perpendicular—perpendicularity comes later (Days 14–15). They're adjacent, sharing a boundary, perfectly accounting for the full dimension of each ambient space: $r + (n-r) = n$ on the left, $r + (m-r) = m$ on the right. Everything that enters gets routed to one of two destinations: either it maps to something in the column space, or it vanishes in the null space. This is the most important picture for intuition: it shows why knowing $r$ immediately determines all four dimensions, and why there are no "leftover" dimensions—each space is a complete partition of its ambient world.

**Picture 2: Rank as the Bottleneck.**  
Imagine a highway with $n$ lanes entering a tunnel and only $m$ lanes exiting. Inside the tunnel, the matrix $A$ acts as a lane-merger and lane-killer. It narrows everything down to exactly $r$ "passing" lanes. These $r$ lanes emerge on the other side into the output highway. The other $(n-r)$ input lanes are "killed"—they get routed to the null space, where they contribute nothing to the output. From the output perspective: of the $m$ output lanes available, only $r$ of them are actually active (fed by the tunnel); the other $(m-r)$ exit lanes are dead (part of the left null space—they can't be reached by any input). The number $r$ is the bottleneck—it's the maximum traffic that can flow through. It simultaneously counts the effective input lanes and the active output lanes. You can't have more active outputs than $r$, and you can't have more than $r$ independent inputs produce different outputs. The pivot count forces the same constraint on both sides. Think of $r$ as the "information capacity" of the matrix: no matter which direction you measure, it's the same—the matrix transmits exactly $r$ units of independent information, no more, no less.

**Picture 3 (The "coordinate" perspective).**  
Another way to see why row rank equals column rank: imagine you're trying to describe $\mathbb{R}^m$ with coordinates (like latitude and longitude describe Earth). If the column space of $A$ has dimension $r$, then you need exactly $r$ numbers to specify any point in $C(A)$. Those $r$ numbers correspond to the $r$ free variables in the solutions to $Ax = b$ (one for each pivot column). Now, from the row perspective: the row space has dimension $r$, meaning you need $r$ independent directions to span it. These $r$ independent row directions correspond to the $r$ pivot rows. The correspondence is exact—you don't get a mismatch. This is why row rank must equal column rank: they're two descriptions of the same $r$-dimensional "active zone" where the matrix actually does its work. Both row and column spaces are windows into the same $r$-dimensional substructure hidden inside the matrix.

## Concrete-first walkthrough

**Definition 6.1 — One matrix, four shadows.**  
An $m \times n$ matrix $A$ throws four shadows—four fundamental subspaces that capture everything about how $A$ behaves:
- **Column space** $C(A)$: all vectors reachable as $Ax$ for any choice of $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$. This is the image or range of $A$—all the outputs you can possibly produce.
- **Null space** $N(A)$: all solutions to $Ax = 0$, a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. This is the kernel of $A$—all the silent inputs that get mapped to zero.
- **Row space** $C(A^T)$: the column space of $A^T$ (equivalently, the span of $A$'s rows viewed as vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$), a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. These are the "active" input directions.
- **Left null space** $N(A^T)$: all $y$ where $A^Ty = 0$, equivalently $y^T A = 0$ (the $y$'s for which $y$ acting on the left of $A$ gives zero), a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$. This is what output directions don't actually come from any input.

Notice the pairing by ambient space: column space and left null space both live in $\mathbb{R}^m$ (the output/codomain); row space and null space both live in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (the input/domain). Together they partition each space completely.

**Pre-teaching the Transpose: $A^T$ = Rows ↔ Columns.**  
Before we go further, you need to know what $A^T$ means. If $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6\end{pmatrix}$ (2 rows, 3 columns), then $A^T = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6\end{pmatrix}$ (3 rows, 2 columns). The rows of $A$ become the columns of $A^T$. Position $(i,j)$ in $A$ moves to position $(j,i)$ in $A^T$. That's the whole definition.

Why does this matter? Because $C(A^T)$ is the column space of $A^T$, which is the same as the *row space* of $A$. The rows of $A$ are the columns of $A^T$. Since columns are what span the column space, the columns of $A^T$ (which are $A$'s rows) span the row space of $A$. This is how Definition 6.1's notation avoids inventing a new symbol for row space—it just uses $C(A^T)$.

One key rule appears in the proof of Day 6 Exercise 7: if $A$ is $m \times n$ and $B$ is $n \times p$, then $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$—the product transposes *in reverse order* (think "socks-shoes": you put socks on first, then shoes; to undo it, you remove shoes first, then socks). This reversal happens because rows and columns trade roles when you transpose.

**Lemma 6.1 — Row rank equals column rank, and both equal the pivot count.**  
Let $U$ be any row-echelon form of $A$ with $r$ pivots. Then:
- (a) $\dim(\text{row space of } A) = r$.
- (b) $\dim(C(A)) = r$.

This lemma is the crown jewel: it says the pivot count tells you *both* the row dimension and the column dimension. Why is this shocking? Because rows and columns seem like different things—one lives horizontally, one vertically. But the same number $r$ governs both. The proof is the day's hardest argument, so pay attention.

**Key idea**: elimination doesn't change column dependencies. Since $U = EA$ with $E$ invertible, we have $Ax = 0 \iff Ux = 0$—they have the same null space. And a null-space vector is exactly a recipe for column dependence: if $Ax = 0$, then $\sum_{j} x_j a_j = 0$ (where $a_j$ is column $j$ of $A$), meaning the columns depend with coefficients $x_j$. So if columns of $A$ are dependent, the same dependency holds in $U$. Conversely, if they're independent in $U$, they were independent in $A$.

In $U$, the pivot columns are visibly independent: you can read it off the echelon form—each pivot row has a leading 1 in a unique pivot column. And every non-pivot column of $U$ is a combination of earlier pivot columns to its left (by the echelon property). Therefore, the $r$ pivot columns of $A$ are independent and span $C(A)$—they form a basis. So $\dim(C(A)) = r$.

The row rank is immediate from Day 5 (Theorem 5.2): the $r$ nonzero rows of $U$ are independent (you verify this by looking at each pivot column in turn—the leading 1's force independence) and their span is unchanged from $A$'s row space (because row operations don't change what the rows span). So $\dim(\text{row space}) = r$.

**Theorem 6.1 — Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, Part 1: The Dimension Ledger.**  
Let $A$ be $m \times n$ with $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$. Then:
- $\dim(\text{row space}) = \dim(C(A)) = r$.
- $\dim(N(A)) = n - r$.
- $\dim(N(A^T)) = m - r$.

This theorem wraps up Days 4, 5, and 6 in a clean package. Once you know $r$, you know *all four* dimensions instantly. You don't need to solve any systems, row-reduce again, or do any new work—plug in $r$ and the formulas spit out the answer.

Where does this come from? It's not new machinery. Statement 1 is Lemma 6.1. For statement 2, view $A$ as a linear map $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Day 4's rank–nullity theorem says $\dim(\ker T) + \dim(\operatorname{im} T) = \dim(\text{domain})$. Applying it here: $\dim(N(A)) + \dim(C(A)) = n$. Since $\dim(C(A)) = r$ by Lemma 6.1, we get $\dim(N(A)) = n - r$. For statement 3, apply the same argument to the matrix $A^T$ (viewed as the map $\mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$): rank–nullity gives $\dim(N(A^T)) + \dim(C(A^T)) = m$, and since the row space $C(A^T)$ has dimension $r$, we get $\dim(N(A^T)) = m - r$.

Notice the symmetry: domain gets split into row space $(r)$ plus null space $(n-r) = n$ total. Codomain gets split into column space $(r)$ plus left null space $(m-r) = m$ total. Perfect accounting, no dimension lost. Every part is assembly, not invention: you're plugging Lemma 6.1 and Day 5's pivot count into Day 4's rank–nullity theorem, applied twice.

**The synthesis: Why today matters.**  
Day 6 ties together everything you learned in Days 1–5. Day 1 taught you subspaces; Day 4 gave you rank–nullity; Day 5 showed you how to find pivots and rank via row reduction. Today, you see that these three separate ideas are actually describing the *same object* from different angles. The pivot count from Day 5 is the magic number that determines all four dimensions. Row rank and column rank are not two separate facts—they're the same number $r$ observed from two different directions. The null-space dimension isn't a mystery—it's just $n - r$, by rank–nullity applied to a map. This is the moment where linear algebra "clicks": what seemed like four separate definitions is actually one unified structure, all controlled by the single rank $r$. Every computation—finding pivots, understanding column dependencies, predicting solution-space shapes—flows from this one unified principle. Memorize $r$; everything else cascades.

**Memory anchors to hold this together.**  
When you encounter a matrix problem asking for dimensions or subspace bases, use this protocol:

(1) Row-reduce $A$ to find pivots; this gives you $r$ immediately.

(2) The $r$ nonzero rows of the row-echelon form are a basis for the row space—dimension is $r$. Read them directly off the echelon form; no new computation.

(3) The $r$ pivot columns of the *original* $A$ are a basis for the column space—dimension is $r$. Go back to $A$ (not the row-reduced form), and grab the columns where pivots sat.

(4) The solutions to $Ax = 0$ give $N(A)$; dimension is $n - r$ by rank–nullity, no new work needed. Solve using the row-echelon form, but don't recompute; just use the formula.

(5) The solutions to $A^Ty = 0$ give $N(A^T)$; dimension is $m - r$ by rank–nullity applied to $A^T$. Same process, applied to the transpose.

One row reduction. Four dimensions. No contradictions. That's the power of Theorem 6.1.

**Why this matters for solving linear systems.**  
Every system $Ax = b$ is really a question about whether $b \in C(A)$. If $b$ is in the column space, the system is consistent (has at least one solution). If not, it's inconsistent. The column space is the "reachable set"—everything $A$ can produce. The left null space captures the opposite: it's the set of constraints (linear combinations of rows) that must be satisfied for consistency. The dimension formulas tell you exactly how much freedom you have. With $r$ pivots, you have $r$ constrained variables and $(n-r)$ free variables in any solution. This structure is completely determined by the rank—knowing $r$ lets you predict the solution space's shape before solving anything.

## Proof roadmaps

**Lemma 6.1 — The key proof.** (Why column rank equals row rank.)  
The trick is that elimination preserves null spaces, which encode column dependencies. If two columns are dependent in $A$, they remain dependent in $U$; if they're independent in $U$, they were independent in $A$.

*Step 1 (Row rank):* This part is just Day 5 (Theorem 5.2) restated: $\dim(\text{row space}) = r$. The $r$ nonzero rows of $U$ are linearly independent and span the row space. You verify independence by reading the pivot positions: in echelon form, each pivot row has a unique leading 1 in its pivot column, and columns after leading 1's contain zeros in earlier rows—so no nonzero row can be a combination of earlier rows. Therefore the rows are linearly independent. And row operations don't change the span of the rows (they only replace rows with their combinations), so row space$(A) =$ row space$(U)$. Thus the row rank is $r$.

*Step 2 (Column space independence):* To show $\dim(C(A)) = r$ as well, you need to prove the pivot columns of $A$ are independent. The key insight is using the null-space equivalence. Suppose some nontrivial combination of the pivot columns of $A$ equals zero: $\sum_{i \in \text{pivots}} c_i a_i = 0$ where $a_i$ is column $i$ of $A$ and not all $c_i = 0$. Build $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with these coefficients $c_i$ in the pivot positions and zeros in all non-pivot positions. Then $Ax = \sum_{i \in \text{pivots}} c_i a_i = 0$, so $x$ is in $N(A)$, which means $Ux = 0$ as well (using $N(A) = N(U)$). Now restrict your attention to just the pivot rows and pivot columns: you have an $r \times r$ system that's upper-triangular with the pivots on the diagonal. Since the pivots are nonzero, this upper-triangular matrix is invertible—its only solution to $Ux = 0$ on those coordinates is $x = 0$. Contradiction. So the pivot columns of $A$ are linearly independent.

*Step 3 (Column space spanning):* To show they span $C(A)$, you show every non-pivot column of $A$ is a linear combination of the pivot columns. By the echelon form structure, every non-pivot column of $U$ is forced to be a combination of earlier pivot columns (by the leading 1's in pivot columns). For a non-pivot column $j$, read off the coefficients: column $j$ of $U = \sum_k \lambda_k (\text{column } k_{\text{pivot}} \text{ of } U)$ with $\lambda_k$ the entries above the pivot in column $j$ of $U$. Build $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $-1$ in position $j$, the coefficients $\lambda_k$ in the pivot positions $k$, and zeros elsewhere: by construction, $Ux = 0$ (the non-pivot column becomes a combination of pivot columns). Therefore $Ax = 0$ as well. Rearranging $Ax = 0$, you get that non-pivot column of $A$ equals exactly the same combination of $A$'s pivot columns as in $U$. So every non-pivot column of $A$ is a combination of pivot columns.

Conclusion: the $r$ pivot columns of $A$ are linearly independent and span $C(A)$, so they form a basis. Therefore $\dim(C(A)) = r$, and we've proven: row rank = column rank = pivot count.

**Theorem 6.1 — Assembly proof.** (All four dimensions.)  
- *Part 1:* Lemma 6.1 directly gives $\dim(\text{row space}) = \dim(C(A)) = r$.
- *Part 2:* Apply Day 4's rank–nullity theorem to the linear map $T_A: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ defined by $T_A(x) = Ax$. The kernel is $N(A)$ and the image is $C(A)$. Since the domain has dimension $n$, rank–nullity says $\dim(N(A)) + \dim(C(A)) = n$. Plugging in $\dim(C(A)) = r$ from Lemma 6.1 gives $\dim(N(A)) = n - r$.
- *Part 3:* Apply Day 4's rank–nullity to the matrix $A^T$ viewed as a linear map $T_{A^T}: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$ defined by $T_{A^T}(y) = A^Ty$. The kernel is $N(A^T)$ and the image is $C(A^T)$ = row space of $A$. The domain $\mathbb{R}^m$ has dimension $m$, so rank–nullity gives $\dim(N(A^T)) + \dim(C(A^T)) = m$. Plugging in $\dim(C(A^T)) = r$ from Lemma 6.1 gives $\dim(N(A^T)) = m - r$.

## Flashcards

Use these to test your understanding. Cover the answers before looking.

**Q:** Name the four fundamental subspaces of an $m \times n$ matrix. Where does each one live?  
**A:** $C(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ (column space), $N(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ (null space), $C(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ (row space), $N(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ (left null space).

**Q:** State all four dimensions in terms of $r$, $m$, and $n$.  
**A:** $\dim C(A) = \dim C(A^T) = r$; $\dim N(A) = n - r$; $\dim N(A^T) = m - r$.

**Q:** Why does row rank equal column rank? Answer in one sentence.  
**A:** Elimination preserves null spaces, which encode column dependencies—so the $r$ pivot columns of $A$ form a basis for $C(A)$, proving $\dim(C(A)) = r = \dim(\text{row space})$.

**Q:** What is $A^T$ and state the transpose product rule.  
**A:** $A^T$ is the transpose of $A$: rows become columns and vice versa. If $A$ is $m \times n$ and $B$ is $n \times p$, then $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$ (order reverses).

**Q:** What does $b \in C(A)$ mean for the system $Ax = b$?  
**A:** The system $Ax = b$ is consistent (has at least one solution)—$b$ is in the range of $A$.

**Q:** Describe $N(A^T)$ in words.  
**A:** All $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$ such that $y^T A = 0$—equivalently, all combinations of the rows of $A$ that vanish. Called "left" null space because $y$ multiplies $A$ on the left.

**Q:** On which days do we learn that the four subspaces are orthogonal complements?  
**A:** Days 14–15. Today we only see they pair up and partition each ambient space; perpendicularity comes later.

**Q:** If $A$ is $5 \times 7$ with rank 3, what are the four dimensions?  
**A:** $\dim C(A) = 3$, $\dim N(A) = 7 - 3 = 4$, $\dim C(A^T) = 3$, $\dim N(A^T) = 5 - 3 = 2$.

**Q:** What is Theorem 6.1 in your own words?  
**A:** Once you know the rank $r$, the dimensions of all four subspaces are determined by assembly: row and column spaces are $r$-dimensional (Lemma 6.1), and applying rank–nullity to $A$ and $A^T$ gives the null-space dimensions immediately as $n-r$ and $m-r$.

**Q:** If I want to determine whether a system $Ax = b$ (with $A$ being $5 \times 10$ and rank 3) is consistent, what should I check?  
**A:** Check whether $b$ is in the column space $C(A)$. Since $\dim C(A) = 3$ and $C(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^5$, there are 5-dimensional "constraints" reducing the dimension to 3. Equivalently, $b$ must satisfy the $m - r = 5 - 3 = 2$ independent left null-space equations.
