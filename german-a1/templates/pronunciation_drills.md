# Pronunciation Drills

These are motor-skill drills, not grammar explanations. Vietnamese has no `ü`/`ö`, does not release final consonants, allows almost no consonant clusters, and is a tone language — so German sentence melody has to be trained as a new physical habit, not understood as a rule. Do every drill out loud, daily, for the two minutes given. Speed comes after accuracy, never before it.

Each section below: the sound, why it is hard for a Vietnamese speaker, an 8-word ladder (easy to hard), and a 2-minute daily drill.

## `ü` — /yː/ and /ʏ/

Vietnamese has no rounded front vowel — speakers substitute `i` or `u`. `ü` needs the tongue position of `i` (front, high) with the lips rounded like `u`. Say `i`, then round your lips without moving your tongue.

Word ladder: Tür /tyːɐ̯/, über /ˈyːbɐ/, für /fyːɐ̯/, müde /ˈmyːdə/, Bücher /ˈbyːçɐ/, grün /ɡʁyːn/, fünf /fʏnf/, München /ˈmʏnçən/

**2-minute drill:** Say "i — ü" ten times, holding the lip-rounding step for a full second each time. Then read the ladder aloud twice, slowly, checking in a mirror that your lips stay rounded on every `ü`. Finish with the sentence: "Die fünf Bücher sind für dich." three times.

## `ö` — /øː/ and /œ/

Same substitution problem as `ü`: Vietnamese speakers reach for `o` or `e`. `ö` is the tongue position of `e` (front, mid) with rounded lips.

Word ladder: schön /ʃøːn/, hören /ˈhøːʁən/, können /ˈkœnən/, zwölf /tsvœlf/, Köln /kœln/, Löffel /ˈlœfəl/, öffnen /ˈœfnən/, größer /ˈɡʁøːsɐ/

**2-minute drill:** Say "e — ö" ten times, holding the rounding. Then read the ladder aloud twice. Finish with the sentence: "Können Sie das Fenster öffnen?" three times, keeping the lips rounded through the whole word "können."

## `ch` — both variants — /ç/ and /x/

Vietnamese has no soft `ch` (the sound after `i`, `e` and consonants) and speakers reach for `k` or `s` instead. Soft `ch` (as in ich) is made at the hard palate, like a hissed "h" pushed through a narrow gap.

The hard `ch` (after `a`, `o`, `u`) exists in some Vietnamese dialects and not in others, so check yours first. **Northern Vietnamese:** the `kh` in *không* is already this exact sound — a rasping scrape at the back of the throat. Say *không*, hold the `kh`, and you have the `ch` of `auch`; transfer it, do not build it. **Southern Vietnamese:** your `kh` is a plain `k` with a puff of air, which is not the same sound, so you do have to build it — make it further back, in the throat, like clearing it gently, with the air scraping continuously instead of stopping.

Word ladder — soft /ç/: ich /ɪç/, nicht /nɪçt/, Milch /mɪlç/ — hard /x/: auch /aʊx/, Buch /buːx/, acht /axt/

**2-minute drill:** Alternate "ich — auch" ten times, feeling the point of contact move from the front of the mouth (soft) to the back of the throat (hard). Then read all six ladder words aloud twice. Finish with: "Ich möchte auch ein Buch." three times.

## Uvular `r` — /ʁ/ and /ɐ/

Vietnamese `r` is either a flap or an approximant made with the tongue tip; German `r` is made further back, at the uvula, and in many positions is barely audible — closer to a vowel colouring than a consonant. Do not use the Vietnamese tongue-tip `r`.

Word ladder: rot /ʁoːt/, Frau /fʁaʊ/, drei /dʁaɪ/, Brot /bʁoːt/, Uhr /uːɐ̯/, Jahr /jaːɐ̯/

**2-minute drill:** Gargle water for 5 seconds to feel where the uvula is, then dry-gargle (no water) to make a soft uvular buzz five times. Read the ladder aloud twice, keeping `r` short and back in the throat, almost swallowed at the end of a word (Uhr, Jahr). Finish with: "Die Frau trinkt um drei Uhr Kaffee." three times.

## Final consonant release

German word endings give a Vietnamese speaker two separate problems, and they need two separate drills. Vietnamese allows only `p t k m n ng` at the end of a syllable, and it never releases them.

### Final stops — release them — /p/ /t/ /k/

Vietnamese final consonants are unreleased (the mouth stops the sound without a puff of air). German final consonants must be released clearly, and voiced consonants at the end of a word devoice (`d` becomes a `t` sound, `g` becomes a `k` sound). These finals exist in Vietnamese; only the release is missing.

Word ladder: und /ʊnt/, Kind /kɪnt/, Hand /hant/, gut /ɡuːt/, Bett /bɛt/, weg /vɛk/

**2-minute drill:** Say each ladder word and add a tiny puff of air after the final consonant, as if the word had a silent extra syllable. Read the ladder aloud twice this way, then at normal speed. Finish with: "Das Kind gibt mir die Hand und geht weg." three times, releasing every final consonant.

### Finals Vietnamese does not have at all — /s/ /l/ /f/ /ç/ /x/ /ts/

These are the harder half. Vietnamese has no syllable-final `s`, `l`, `f`, `ch` or `ts`, so the instinct is either to drop the sound or to rescue it with an extra vowel — "Hau-sờ" for `Haus`, "Ho-ten" for `Hotel`. Both are wrong: the consonant is the last thing in the word, and nothing follows it.

Word ladder: Haus /haʊs/, alles /ˈaləs/, viel /fiːl/, Hotel /hoˈtɛl/, Brief /bʁiːf/, jetzt /jɛtst/, nichts /nɪçts/, Buch /buːx/

**2-minute drill:** Say each word, then say it again holding the final consonant for a full second (`Hausssss`, `vielllll`) so you can hear that it is really there and that no vowel follows it. Read the ladder twice that way, then twice at normal speed with a normal-length ending. Finish with "Ich schreibe jetzt einen Brief." and "Das Haus ist nicht weit vom Hotel." three times each.

## Clusters — /ʃp/ /ʃt/ /ʃpʁ/ /ʃv/

Vietnamese syllables allow at most one consonant at the start or end; German freely stacks three or four (`spr`, `schw`, `rbst`). The instinct is to insert a vowel between them — resist it.

Word ladder: Straße /ˈʃtʁaːsə/, sprechen /ˈʃpʁɛçən/, Stuhl /ʃtuːl/, schwarz /ʃvaʁts/, Herbst /hɛʁpst/, sprichst /ʃpʁɪçst/

**2-minute drill:** Build each cluster one consonant at a time before saying the full word: "s — sp — spr — sprechen." Do this for `sprechen`, `schwarz`, and `sprichst`. Then read the full ladder aloud twice at normal speed, no inserted vowels. Finish with "Sprichst du Deutsch?" and "Der Herbst ist schön." three times each.

## Intonation vs. Vietnamese tone

Vietnamese is a tone language: pitch on a single syllable changes word meaning. German has no lexical tone — pitch instead marks the *whole sentence* as a statement, a W-question, or a yes/no question. Vietnamese-L1 speakers often carry syllable-level tone habits into German, which makes sentences sound choppy or oddly emphasised. Practise pitch as a smooth line over the whole sentence, not a target on one syllable.

**Statement — falls at the end:**
- Ich heiße Anna. ↘
- Ich komme aus Vietnam. ↘
- Das ist mein Bruder. ↘

**W-question (wer, wie, was, wo, wann) — falls at the end:**
- Wie heißen Sie? ↘
- Wo wohnst du? ↘
- Was trinken Sie gern? ↘

**Yes/no question — rises at the end:**
- Kommst du aus Köln? ↗
- Sprechen Sie Deutsch? ↗
- Ist das dein Bruder? ↗

**2-minute drill:** Read each group of three aloud, exaggerating the pitch direction with your hand (hand drops for falling, hand rises for rising). Then read all nine sentences again at normal speed, keeping the exaggerated pitch line but a natural volume.
