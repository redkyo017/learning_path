# Pronunciation Reference

## 1. Why German is easier than English here

German spelling tells you the pronunciation. Unlike English — where *though*, *through*, *tough* and *thought* all spell "ough" differently — German letters keep the same sound almost everywhere, so once you know the twenty-two rules in section 3 you can say a German word correctly the first time you see it, without a dictionary and without pronunciation printed on your flashcards.

Read this page once, properly and out loud, in Phase 0. After that it is reference: come back to section 3 when a spelling surprises you, and to section 5 when your speaking feels stuck.

**How the notation is written here.** The symbols in slashes are IPA — the International Phonetic Alphabet, one symbol per sound, used by every dictionary in the world. `ː` means the vowel is long. `ˈ` sits immediately before the stressed syllable. To keep the tables readable this page writes `/ts/` rather than the tie-bar `/t͡s/`, and `/aɪ/` rather than `/aɪ̯/`; de.wiktionary.org uses the fuller forms, and they mean the same thing.

## 2. The German sound inventory

### Vowels

German pairs almost every vowel: a long one and a short one, spelled with the same letter. The long/short difference changes meaning (*Stadt* vs. *Staat*), so it is not decoration.

## 3. The spelling-to-sound decoder

Twenty-two rules. Apply them left to right on any new word and you will be right nearly every time.

**1. `ei` = /aɪ/.** mein /maɪn/, drei /dʁaɪ/, Zeit /tsaɪt/, heißen /ˈhaɪsən/.

**2. `ie` = /iː/.** Liebe /ˈliːbə/, sieben /ˈziːbən/, vier /fiːɐ̯/, viel /fiːl/.

*The mnemonic that actually works:* say the **English name of the second letter**. `ei` — second letter is *i*, English name "eye" → /aɪ/. `ie` — second letter is *e*, English name "ee" → /iː/. It works for both, every time. This is the single most common beginner error: *drei* is "dry", not "dree"; *vier* is "feer", not "fire".

**3. `eu` and `äu` = /ɔʏ/.** neun /nɔʏn/, Euro /ˈɔʏʁo/, Häuser /ˈhɔʏzɐ/, Bäume /ˈbɔʏmə/. Not "eh-oo" and not "ay-oo" — one glided sound.

**4. `au` = /aʊ/.** Haus /haʊs/, Frau /fʁaʊ/, auch /aʊx/.

**5. `ch` after i, e, ä, ö, ü, a diphthong or a consonant = /ç/.** ich /ɪç/, nicht /nɪçt/, Milch /mɪlç/, möchte /ˈmœçtə/. *Vietnamese trap:* the reflex is to substitute `k` ("ik") or `s` ("is"). Both are heard as wrong immediately, and *ich* is in every second sentence you will say in the exam.

**6. `ch` after a, o, u or au = /x/.** auch /aʊx/, Buch /buːx/, Nacht /naxt/, acht /axt/. Northern Vietnamese speakers already own this sound as the `kh` of *không*.

**7. `sch` = /ʃ/.** Schule /ˈʃuːlə/, schön /ʃøːn/, waschen /ˈvaʃən/. Three letters, one sound.

**8. `sp-` and `st-` at the start of a word or word-stem = /ʃp/ and /ʃt/.** sprechen /ˈʃpʁɛçən/, Sport /ʃpɔʁt/, Straße /ˈʃtʁaːsə/, Student /ʃtuˈdɛnt/. Inside a word they stay /sp/ /st/: Fenster /ˈfɛnstɐ/, ist /ɪst/.

**9. Final `-b`, `-d`, `-g` devoice to /p/, /t/, /k/.** Rad /ʁaːt/ sounds identical to Rat /ʁaːt/; Tag /taːk/; halb /halp/; Kind /kɪnt/; und /ʊnt/. This also applies before a consonant: *gibt* /ɡiːpt/. Add an ending and the voicing comes back: Räder /ˈʁɛːdɐ/, Tage /ˈtaːɡə/.

**10. Final `-er` = /ɐ/, a vowel, not a consonant.** Vater /ˈfaːtɐ/, Mutter /ˈmʊtɐ/, aber /ˈaːbɐ/, Kinder /ˈkɪndɐ/. *Vietnamese trap:* rolling or flapping a tongue-tip `r` here. There is no r-consonant in these words at all — the ending is a weak "uh" close to Vietnamese `ơ`. The same applies to `r` after a long vowel: Uhr /uːɐ̯/, Jahr /jaːɐ̯/, wir /viːɐ̯/.

**11. `v` = /f/ in native words.** Vater /ˈfaːtɐ/, vier /fiːɐ̯/, viel /fiːl/, von /fɔn/. In borrowed words it is /v/: Vase /ˈvaːzə/, Video /ˈviːdeo/. At A1 the native words are far more frequent, so /f/ is the safe default.

**12. `w` = /v/.** Wasser /ˈvasɐ/, wo /voː/, wir /viːɐ̯/, wohnen /ˈvoːnən/. Never the English "w".

**13. `z` and `tz` = /ts/.** Zeit /tsaɪt/, zehn /tseːn/, Katze /ˈkatsə/, jetzt /jɛtst/. *Vietnamese trap:* simplifying to `s` ("sait") or `t` ("tait"). Both consonants must be there, in that order, with nothing between them.

**14. `s` before a vowel = /z/; everywhere else = /s/.** Voiced: Sonne /ˈzɔnə/, sieben /ˈziːbən/, sagen /ˈzaːɡən/, lesen /ˈleːzən/. Unvoiced: Haus /haʊs/, ist /ɪst/, Bus /bʊs/.

**15. `ß` and `ss` = /s/, always unvoiced.** Straße /ˈʃtʁaːsə/, heißen /ˈhaɪsən/, essen /ˈɛsən/, müssen /ˈmʏsən/. The choice between them is itself a length signal — see rules 16 and 17.

**16. A vowel is LONG if it is doubled, followed by `h`, or followed by one consonant.** Doubled: Boot /boːt/, See /zeː/, Saal /zaːl/. Vowel + h (the h is silent, it only marks length): ihn /iːn/, Uhr /uːɐ̯/, wohnen /ˈvoːnən/, gehen /ˈɡeːən/. Vowel + one consonant: gut /ɡuːt/, Tag /taːk/, kam /kaːm/. A vowel before `ß` is long: Straße /ˈʃtʁaːsə/, groß /ɡʁoːs/.

**17. A vowel is SHORT if two or more consonants follow it.** Mann /man/, kommen /ˈkɔmən/, bitte /ˈbɪtə/, Fluss /flʊs/, Herbst /hɛʁpst/. *Vietnamese trap:* Vietnamese vowel length is not written this way, so the eye ignores the doubled consonant. It changes meaning — *Stadt* /ʃtat/ (city) vs. *Staat* /ʃtaːt/ (state). A handful of very common short words break rule 16: das /das/, was /vas/, mit /mɪt/, ist /ɪst/, bin /bɪn/, an /an/, in /ɪn/, um /ʊm/. Learn those eight as exceptions and the rule holds everywhere else.

**18. Final `-ig` = /ɪç/, not /ɪk/.** richtig /ˈʁɪçtɪç/, wichtig /ˈvɪçtɪç/, zwanzig /ˈtsvantsɪç/, billig /ˈbɪlɪç/. Add an ending and it becomes a normal /ɡ/: richtige /ˈʁɪçtɪɡə/.

**19. `h` at the start of a word or syllable is pronounced; after a vowel it is silent.** Pronounced: Haus /haʊs/, haben /ˈhaːbən/, Hotel /hoˈtɛl/. Silent: Uhr /uːɐ̯/, sehen /ˈzeːən/, Jahr /jaːɐ̯/.

**20. `qu` = /kv/, and `-tion` = /tsi̯oːn/ with the stress on it.** Quittung /ˈkvɪtʊŋ/, Nation /naˈtsi̯oːn/, Station /ʃtaˈtsi̯oːn/. Never the English "kw" or "shun".

**21. Word stress falls on the first syllable of native German words.** Arbeit /ˈaʁbaɪt/, Wasser /ˈvasɐ/, Mutter /ˈmʊtɐ/, Bücher /ˈbyːçɐ/. Borrowed words often break this and stress a later syllable: Student /ʃtuˈdɛnt/, Hotel /hoˈtɛl/, Musik /muˈziːk/, Papier /paˈpiːɐ̯/.

**22. A separable prefix takes the stress; an inseparable prefix never does.** Separable — stress on the prefix: aufstehen /ˈaʊfʃteːən/, einkaufen /ˈaɪnkaʊfən/, ankommen /ˈankɔmən/. Inseparable (be-, ge-, ver-, er-, ent-, emp-, zer-) — stress on the stem, and the prefix vowel shrinks to /ə/ or /ɛ/: besuchen /bəˈzuːxən/, verstehen /fɛɐ̯ˈʃteːən/, Geschäft /ɡəˈʃɛft/. *Vietnamese trap:* Vietnamese gives every syllable roughly equal weight and its own tone, so German unstressed syllables come out too strong and too even. German stressed syllables are longer and louder, and everything around them is squeezed.

## 4. How to look up any word

Go to **https://de.wiktionary.org** and type the word into the search box. Every entry gives you, for free, in one place:

- **Aussprache** — the pronunciation. You will see `IPA: […]` followed by the transcription in square brackets, and next to it **Hörbeispiele** with a small play button: that is a recording by a native speaker. Listen before you decide you have read the IPA correctly.
- The **article and the plural**, in the grey box at the top of the entry. The row labelled *Singular / Plural* under *Nominativ* gives you `der Tisch / die Tische` — the two things you must memorise with every noun.
- **Bedeutungen** — meanings, in German. At A1 you will not read these; use the IPA, the audio and the grey box, and get the English meaning from your Wortliste.
- **Worttrennung** — where the word splits across syllables. The stress mark `ˈ` in the IPA tells you which of those syllables is the loud one.

The interface is in German, but you only need those four words. **This is why no vocabulary card in this path prints the pronunciation:** any of your 650 words is fifteen seconds away, with real audio attached, which is better than any transcription written on a card. Look up a word the first time you meet it, say it aloud three times with the audio, and move on.

Two further dictionaries, both verified:

- **https://www.dwds.de** — German-to-German, with real sentences from a large corpus showing how a word is actually used. Useful from Phase 2 onward.
- **https://www.dict.cc** — German to English and back, shows noun gender, has community-recorded audio. The fastest option when you need the English meaning as well as the sound.

### Unverified — check these yourself before relying on them

These are well-known sites, but they could not be confirmed working from this environment. Try them; if one does not load, drop it and stay with the verified list above.

- https://forvo.com/languages/de/ — many different native speakers pronouncing the same single word, so you hear the range rather than one voice.
- https://dict.leo.org/german-english/ — dictionary with a usage forum.

## 5. The ten sounds that will cost you marks

Ranked by how much damage they do in the Sprechen module. Each one has a drill in `templates/pronunciation_drills.md` — the rank tells you where to spend your two minutes when you only have two minutes.

| # | Sound | Why it costs you | Drill |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | /yː/ and /ʏ/ — `ü` | Vietnamese has no rounded front vowel, so it comes out as `i` or `u`. It is in *für*, *fünf*, *über*, *müssen*, *Tür* — words you cannot avoid in a single exam answer. | `ü` |
| 2 | /øː/ and /œ/ — `ö` | Same missing sound type. *schön*, *können*, *möchte*, *zwölf* — and *möchte* is the backbone of Sprechen Teil 3. | `ö` |
| 3 | /ç/ — soft `ch` | No Vietnamese equivalent, and it sits in *ich*, the most frequent word you will say. Substituting `k` or `s` marks every sentence. Southern speakers must also build /x/ (*auch*, *Buch*); Northern speakers already have it as the `kh` of *không*. | `ch` — both variants |
| 4 | /ʁ/ — uvular `r` | The Vietnamese tongue-tip `r` is audibly the wrong sound in *rot*, *Frau*, *drei*, *Brot*, and it appears at the start of hundreds of words. | Uvular `r` |
| 5 | Released and devoiced final stops | Vietnamese never releases a final consonant, so *und*, *Kind*, *Hand*, *gut* arrive half-finished and the examiner hears an unfinished word rather than an accent. Rule 9 above is the devoicing half. | Final stops — release them |
| 6 | Word-final /s/, /l/, /f/, /ç/, /ts/ | Vietnamese has none of these at the end of a syllable, so the reflex is to drop them or rescue them with an extra vowel — "Hau-sờ" for *Haus*. Adding a syllable changes the word more than mispronouncing a sound does. | Finals Vietnamese does not have at all |
| 7 | /ɐ/ — final `-er` | Producing a real r in *Vater*, *Mutter*, *aber*, *Kinder* is the most common single tell of a Vietnamese accent in German, and these endings are everywhere. Rule 10 above. | Uvular `r` (the "almost swallowed at the end of a word" half) |
| 8 | /z/ vs. /s/, and /ts/ | Three distinct sounds spelled with `s` and `z`. *Sonne* /ˈzɔnə/ needs voice, *Haus* /haʊs/ does not, *Zeit* /tsaɪt/ needs both consonants. Rules 13 and 14. Northern speakers already own /z/ as the `d` of *da*. | Finals Vietnamese does not have at all |
| 9 | Consonant clusters | Vietnamese allows one consonant per edge; German stacks three or four. Inserting a vowel — "sờ-prechen" — turns one word into two and costs you comprehensibility, not just accent. | Clusters |
| 10 | Sentence intonation | Vietnamese pitch belongs to the syllable; German pitch belongs to the whole sentence. Carrying tone habits across makes you sound choppy, and a yes/no question that does not rise may not be understood as a question at all. | Intonation vs. Vietnamese tone |

**Also worth guarding: vowel length.** It has no drill of its own because it is a reading habit rather than a motor skill — but *Stadt* and *Staat*, *Miete* and *Mitte* are different words, and the difference is only length. Decoder rules 16 and 17 tell you which one you are looking at; apply them every time you read a new word aloud.
