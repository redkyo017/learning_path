# Glossary

Plain-English explanations of every grammar, exam, Anki and study-method term used in this path. Each entry is one sentence, written without grammar jargon, so you can look a term up the first time you meet it in a day file and move on. Grammar terms include a short German example.

## Grammar

| Term | Plain English | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Artikel | The small word before a German noun (der, die, or das) that shows which of three fixed, arbitrary groups the noun belongs to, so you learn it by heart together with the noun rather than by logic. | der Tisch, die Lampe, das Buch |
| Nomen | A word for a person, place, thing or idea, always written with a capital first letter in German. | das Kind (the child) |
| Verb | A doing word that shows an action or a state. | gehen (to go) |
| Konjugation | The way a German verb's ending changes depending on who is doing the action, the same way English changes "go" to "goes" for he or she. | ich gehe, er geht |
| Nominativ | The everyday, default form a noun (and its der/die/das) takes when that noun is the one doing the action in the sentence. | Der Mann schläft. (The man is sleeping.) |
| Akkusativ | A small change some articles make when a noun is having something done to it; at this level you memorise it as part of a whole chunk of words, not from a table of rules. | Ich habe einen Bruder. |
| Dativ | Another small change some articles make, most often paired with certain fixed words like "mit"; at this level you memorise these as whole chunks too, not as a table to learn by heart. | mit dem Bus |
| Perfekt | The everyday way Germans talk about the past in conversation, built from a helper verb (haben or sein) plus a special past form of the main verb. | Ich habe gegessen. (I ate / have eaten.) |
| Partizip II | The special past form of a verb used inside the Perfekt, often starting with "ge-". | gegessen, gemacht, gegangen |
| Modalverb | A helper verb like "can," "want," or "must" that changes the meaning of the main verb, which then gets pushed to the end of the sentence. | Ich muss arbeiten. (I have to work.) |
| trennbares Verb | A verb whose small extra piece breaks off and jumps to the end of the sentence when you use it in a normal sentence. | Ich stehe um sieben Uhr auf. (I get up at seven.) |
| Imperativ | The command form of a verb, used to tell someone to do something. | Komm her! (Come here!) |
| Umlaut | The two little dots (¨) that can sit above a, o, or u and change how the letter sounds, and that sometimes appear when a word changes form. | Mutter → Mütter |
| Plural | The form of a noun that shows there is more than one; in German it is unpredictable, so you memorise it together with each new word. | das Kind → die Kinder |
| Possessivpronomen | A small word like "my," "your," or "his" that shows who owns something. | mein Buch (my book) |
| W-Frage | A question that starts with a question word such as "was," "wer," or "wo" and asks for specific information rather than yes or no. | Wo wohnst du? (Where do you live?) |
| Ja/Nein-Frage | A question that can only be answered with "yes" or "no," made by putting the verb first. | Wohnst du in Berlin? (Do you live in Berlin?) |

## Exam

| Term | Plain English | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hören | The listening part of the exam, where you hear German audio and answer questions about it. | listening to a short announcement and picking the correct answer |
| Lesen | The reading part of the exam, where you read German texts and answer questions about them. | reading a short notice and deciding if a statement is true or false |
| Schreiben | The writing part of the exam, where you write short German texts such as messages or forms. | writing a short email to a friend |
| Sprechen | The speaking part of the exam, normally taken together with other candidates rather than alone, in front of the examiners — the exact number of candidates in the room varies by exam centre, so expect either a pair or a small group. You introduce yourself and then speak mostly to the other candidates rather than to the examiners. | introducing yourself, then asking another candidate a question from a keyword card |
| Teil | A numbered section inside one of the four exam parts. | Teil 1, Teil 2 |
| Prüfung | The exam itself — the official test you take to prove your German level. | die Prüfung am Goethe-Institut |
| Übungssatz | A full practice version of the exam that you complete under timed conditions to rehearse the real thing. | working through a full timed Übungssatz — 65 minutes written (Hören 20, Lesen 25, Schreiben 20) plus about 15 minutes Sprechen |
| Wortliste | The official list of words the exam board expects you to already know. | the Goethe-Institut A1 Wortliste |
| Buchstabieren | Spelling a word out loud letter by letter, which can be tested in the speaking part. | "Wie schreibt man das? B-U-C-H." |
| Modellsatz | An official sample exam, published by the exam board, showing exactly what the real test looks like. | the Goethe-Institut A1 Modellsatz |
| Prüfungszentrum | The physical location or institution where you register for and sit the exam. | booking a seat at your local Prüfungszentrum |

## Anki

| Term | Plain English | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| new card | A flashcard you have never studied before, which Anki shows you for the very first time. | seeing "der Apfel" for the first time |
| review | A flashcard you already learned that Anki is showing you again to check you still remember it. | Anki asks you to recall a word you learned three days ago |
| mature card | A flashcard you have gotten right so many times that Anki now waits weeks or months before showing it again. | a card you have not seen for 40 days because you keep getting it right |
| leech | A flashcard you keep getting wrong over and over, which Anki flags so you can fix or rewrite it. | a card Anki tags after you fail it eight times |
| interval | The number of days Anki waits before showing you a card again. | an interval of 6 days before the next review |
| backlog | The pile of reviews that built up because you did not study on schedule. | 120 overdue cards waiting after a missed week |
| ease | A number Anki keeps for each card that controls how quickly its interval grows after you get it right. | a card's ease dropping after you mark it "Hard" |

## Study method

| Term | Plain English | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| shadowing | Listening to a German recording and speaking along with it at the same time to copy its rhythm and pronunciation. | repeating a Nicos Weg dialogue out loud while it plays |
| minimal pair | Two words that differ by only one sound, practised together so your ear learns to tell them apart. | Miete vs. Mitte |
| chunk | A whole phrase you memorise and use as one piece, without breaking it down into grammar rules. | Ich möchte bitte einen Kaffee. (I would like a coffee, please.) |
| spaced repetition | A study technique where you review information at increasing gaps of time, right before you would otherwise forget it. | reviewing a card after 1 day, then 3 days, then 7 days |
| production vs. recognition | The difference between recalling a word yourself from memory (production) and only recognising it when you see or hear it (recognition). | picking "Brot" out of a list vs. saying "Brot" when asked "bread" |
| IPA | A worldwide alphabet in which every symbol always stands for exactly one sound, used by dictionaries so you can see how a word is said without hearing it. | Wasser is written /ˈvasɐ/ |
| phoneme | One of the sounds a language uses to tell words apart, written between slashes. | /f/ and /v/ are two phonemes, which is why *vier* and *wir* are different words |
| long vs. short vowel | Whether you hold a vowel for longer or cut it off quickly — in German this alone can change one word into another, and a colon in the notation marks the long one. | Stadt /ʃtat/ (city) vs. Staat /ʃtaːt/ (state) |
| diphthong | Two vowels said as one smooth slide inside a single syllable, rather than as two separate beats. | Haus /haʊs/, mein /maɪn/ |
| schwa | The weak, colourless "uh" that German unstressed endings shrink to, written /ə/. | the final e of bitte /ˈbɪtə/ |
| final devoicing | The German habit of hardening b, d and g at the end of a word into p, t and k. | Rad is said /ʁaːt/, exactly like Rat |
| word stress | The one syllable in a word that is said louder and longer than the rest, marked in the notation by a small vertical line in front of it. | Arbeit /ˈaʁbaɪt/ — the first syllable is the loud one |
